## Regional Character

The Tollwood is old, deep, and commercially valuable: oak, beech, and hornbeam over hundreds of square miles, cut for timber and burned for charcoal. The **East Road** runs through it on Concord causeway, and everything the road carries — city goods west, timber and charcoal east — pays somebody a toll somewhere along the way.

The wood has its own body of custom, older than the road and observed even by people who claim not to believe in it. The core of it, the **forest-rules**, is short and universally known: leave an offering at the toll-shrines before travelling; never cut a tree that carries a mark; do not go deep after dark; carry salt. Road-towns keep these rules privately while doing business that quietly violates them, and everyone is aware of the contradiction.

Woodfolk are slow-spoken, watchful, and possessive of local knowledge. An outsider who scoffs at the customs is not argued with — they are simply not helped, which in the deep wood amounts to the same thing as being told to leave. Guides are the region's real export: hiring a good one is the single most useful thing a stranger can do here.

# Hartfell

**Town · ~1,100 people · the forest's only true town**

### First Impression
The East Road runs out of the trees into a clearing, and there is Hartfell: a sprawl of timber halls and sawpits inside a log stockade, woodsmoke and the scream of saws, hides and charcoal and stacked timber bound for the city. Hounds, hard men, laden carts. It feels almost safe after the road — until you notice that the stockade faces *inward*, toward the trees, and that the east gate is barred and hung with offering-marks, and that nobody goes out through it after dark.

### Public Overview
Hartfell is the Tollwood's only town: where East Road trade pools, timber and charcoal are weighed, and city goods are broken down for sale to the wood. The Cinder Ledger runs the sawmill, the timber-weigh, and the local credit; a road-watch of the Reachward Compact patrols the causeway and guards the trade; a chapter of the Mourners' Circle maintains the town's toll-shrine and the older forest observances. It is prosperous, rough, pragmatic, and much more superstitious than it admits to visitors.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Timber-Weigh** — sawmill, weigh-yard, and credit-office; the town's economic heart.
- **The Stockade and East Gate** — the log wall, and the barred, offering-marked gate facing the deep wood.
- **The Antlers** — the big road-inn, hung with hart-skulls; where trade news and forest gossip meet, and where guides are hired.
- **The Coppice Shrine** — the town's toll-shrine, an old offering-stone kept by the Mourners' Circle.
- **Cutters' Row** — woodcutter and charcoal halls; hard, superstitious labour, and the loudest arguments in town.

### Leadership
No single ruler. Hartfell is run by an uneasy partnership between the **timber-factor** (who holds the purse) and the **road-warden captain** (who holds the gate and the watch), with the Mourners' forest-elder commanding moral authority on anything touching the wood. The three do not entirely trust one another, which the townsfolk consider a healthy arrangement.

### Shops and Services
The Antlers (about 5 sp a night, hearty food, forest gossip, guides for hire). The Timber-Weigh: banking, loans, timber and charcoal trade, road supplies, carts and credit east toward the city or west toward the bridge-town. A smith with excellent axes and woodcraft gear, a fletcher, a tanner, and a healer's house offering folk medicine plus a hedge-priest capable of minor healing. The Coppice Shrine performs rites and takes offerings.

### Laws and Customs
A partnership law: the merchant house fines, the road-watch jails. Serious wood-crimes — breaking a toll-shrine, or leading something out of the deep wood toward the road — are judged by the Mourners' Circle and dreaded far more than the watch's justice. The forest-rules are kept in private: offerings before travel, marked trees left standing, salt carried. The east gate is barred at dusk and opened at dawn, for anyone, at any price.

### Local Colour
A cutter-foreman who reads the marks on trees like a legal document; a road-watch sergeant who publicly enforces against the old customs and privately keeps every one of them; a logging crew back a man short and saying nothing about it.

# Coldhearth

**Village · ~200 people · deep-wood charcoal burners**

### First Impression
A day and more off the road, in a clearing of stumps and smoking charcoal-mounds, sits Coldhearth: low turf-roofed huts, racks of drying wood, and the constant blue haze of the burns. The people are lean, watchful, and quiet, marked with charcoal and old scars, and they speak a slow forest-dialect thick with words for things outsiders do not have. Every threshold, every tool, every grave carries an offering-mark. They watch a stranger the way the wood does — patiently, and from all sides.

### Public Overview
Coldhearth burns the Tollwood's charcoal, family by family, and has done so since before anyone's reckoning — the trade that feeds the road-town's smiths and the city's foundries. It keeps the old forest-customs in full where the road-towns keep only fragments. It has no watch, no counting-house, and no interest in either. Outsiders come here for one thing: a guide who can keep them alive deeper in.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Burns** — the charcoal-mounds and drying-racks; the village's life, and a genuinely dangerous place to wander at night.
- **The Elder's Hut** — a turf-hall hung with offering-tokens and old, meticulous maps of the wood.
- **The Mark-Stone** — the village's great toll-shrine, the oldest in the forest; offerings, rites, and the old songs.
- **The Guides' Fire** — where woodsmen gather and guides are hired.

### Leadership
A **forest-elder**, leading by authority of the old customs rather than any office. There is no other authority here; the road-town's factors and the Compact's wardens have no reach at all. Even visiting strangers with real power tread carefully.

### Shops and Services
No inn — a respected guest is given hearth-room and charcoal-stew, and the hospitality is real but earned. Guides into the deep wood are the village's true export: essential, not cheap, and paid in coin, service, or demonstrated respect. Folk healing with herb-lore better than anything in the road-towns. The Mark-Stone for rites and offerings. Charcoal, herb-lore, and woodcraft to trade; no banking, no fine goods, no weapons for sale.

### Laws and Customs
The old customs are law. Offer at the Mark-Stone; keep the bargains; never lead anything out of the deep wood. Breaking a bargain is the one crime the village regards as unforgivable, and it is judged by the elder. Hospitality is sacred — a guest who respects the customs is protected absolutely; one who mocks them is turned out into the dark, which the village considers a fair and complete response.

### Local Colour
A charcoal-foreman who can read a burn by its smoke colour; a young guide who has been deeper than she should have and has gone quiet about it; an elder who knows exactly how much she is willing to tell a stranger, and stops there.

# Tollstone Cross

**Hamlet · ~60 people · a bandit-held road chokepoint**

### First Impression
The East Road runs up to a great broken arch — a ruined Concord toll-station half-swallowed by the wood — and through it, where the causeway pinches between bog and bramble. A clutter of lean-tos, a barred gate of lashed timber, a chain across the road, and hard-eyed folk with bows in the ruin above. A painted board states the toll. Behind the bandit front, an actual little community scrapes by: this is the only stop on three days of road, and everyone knows it.

### Public Overview
Tollstone Cross grew up around the old toll-station at the East Road's worst chokepoint. For a generation it has been held by a company of road-bandits who levy a "toll" on every cart and call it protection — which, against the wood and against rival bandits, it partly is. The compact cannot dislodge them and the merchant houses find it cheaper to pay than to fight, so the toll endures. A handful of honest residents live alongside the bandits because there is nowhere else to live for three days of forest in any direction.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Chain and Gate** — the toll-barrier across the causeway; the bandits' front and their business office.
- **The Broken Arch** — the ruined toll-station above, used as roost and lookout.
- **The Honest Corner** — the few real residents: a smith, an ale-wife, a Mourner who buries the road's dead.
- **The Old Stair** — a sunken stair down into the drowned lower works of the toll-station. The bandits will not go down it and do not like being asked why.

### Leadership
Whoever currently commands the toll company. Authority here is personal and provisional; a toll-boss holds the chain as long as their own people let them, and lieutenants with different ideas about how much violence the trade requires are a permanent feature. The honest residents have no power beyond the bandits' tolerance.

### Shops and Services
The Honest Corner offers a hot meal, ale, and a smith's repair — for coin, on top of the toll. Rough shelter can be bought from the toll company; there is no proper inn and no safe rest by default. The bandits sell passage, rough protection, and reasonably accurate information about the road ahead. No banking, no healing magic. A Mourner will perform rites for the road's dead.

### Laws and Customs
The toll company's law: pay the toll, do not cross the company, do not go down the old stair. A rough order is kept because chaos is bad for the take. Custom — half superstition, half tax — has travellers leave a coin at the Broken Arch "for the road." The honest residents keep their heads down and their opinions to themselves.

### Local Colour
A smith who mends bandit gear all week and hates every minute of it; an ale-wife who knows everyone's business on three days of road; a standoff at the chain that could go four different ways depending on who blinks.
