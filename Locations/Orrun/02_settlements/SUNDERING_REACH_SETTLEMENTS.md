## Regional Character

The Sundering Reach is peat, standing water, grey weather, and old stone. Concord-era causeways cross the fens on raised stonework that predates every living institution; the roads still work, which is more than can be said for most of what the Concord built. The land supports peat-cutting, fowling, reed and thatch, salt trade, mining in the northern heights, and salvage from the ruins the water keeps giving up.

Reach folk are hospitable in a wary, practical way. They put salt at the door-sill and carve or press a small star above the lintel — the star is a Threshold sign for the door the dead go through, and folk here are unfussy about whether it works so long as it is kept up. Funeral custom is taken seriously: the dead are named once aloud, then released. Everyone knows that rites sometimes fail; nobody local claims to know why, and it is considered ill-bred to speculate loudly in someone else's village.

Common institutions across the region. (These are the publicly known bodies of the frontier — their existence, membership, and stated purpose are ordinary common knowledge. Whatever any of them privately wants is a matter for a specific story to decide, and nothing here assumes an answer.)

- **The Reachward Compact** ("the Compact") — a loose regional government of reeves, bailiffs, haywards, and a thin town watch, funded largely by bridge and road tolls. Its reach thins fast the farther you get from the roads.
- **The Mourners' Circle** ("the Mourners' Circle") — a folk death-faith of elders, grave-singers, and shrine-tenders. No central church; each chapter is its own. Widely trusted; often the only authority in a small place.
- **The Ashen Wardens** ("the Ashen Wardens") — a wandering order in ash-grey cloaks who answer calls when a funeral goes wrong. Few in number, always tired, respected everywhere, resident almost nowhere.
- **The Cinder Ledger** ("the Ledger," "the Cinder Ledger") — a large chartered counting-house out of Caradril, which finances the frontier, farms tolls, buys salvage, and sets the price of salt. Prosperous and widely resented.
- **The Gravecallers** — an outlawed, widely feared group rumoured to speak with the dead, blamed for far more than it plausibly does. Mentioned in whispers everywhere; confirmed almost nowhere.

# Hollowmere

**Town · ~900 people · the Reach's largest settlement**

### First Impression
Hollowmere rings a wide hollow of dark, still water. Stilt-houses and peat-smoke crowd the firm ground; planked walkways cross the boggy edges; and out in the flooded basin, when the fog thins, you can see broken grey shapes just under the surface — the tops of something old and enormous. The town smells of peat, brine, and cold water. People are kind but watchful, and every doorway has salt at the sill and a small star above it.

### Public Overview
Founded roughly a century ago on the basin's rim, Hollowmere grew because the edge offered firm ground, peat to cut, fish to net, and ruins to scavenge. It is the seat of the Reachward Compact and the natural hub for the region's roads: east to the bridge-town, west toward the salt trade, south toward the orchard country, north toward the mining heights. Its people are peat-cutters, fishers, coopers, netmakers, and salvagers, and most of them are related to each other.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Rim** — the firm-ground core: market, council hall, the better houses.
- **The Drowned Lantern** — the town inn, on the water's edge; lodging, hot food, and the region's busiest rumor-bench.
- **The Boards** — the plank-walkway stilt district over the bog; poorer, louder, closer to the water.
- **Mourner's Green** — the cemetery and the Mourners' cottage; the town's oldest continuous institution.
- **The Counting-House** — the Cinder Ledger's local seat; loans, salvage-buying, hired work.
- **The Warden Rest** — a modest safe-house kept for the Ashen Wardens.
- **The Basin Shore** — the flooded ruin's edge. Not forbidden, exactly, but nobody goes there for pleasure.

### Leadership
A **reeve** elected by, and answerable to, a small council of householders and trade-heads. The reeve keeps order, hears disputes, and quarrels with the council over money. A handful of watchmen enforce compact law.

### Shops and Services
Lodging and food at the inn; a chandler, a cooper, a fishmonger, a peat-merchant, a hedge-healer with a good herb-cupboard; boats and fen-guides for hire at the shore; the counting-house for loans, appraisal, and paid work. No arcane services and no reliable magical healing — for that, travel south or to the city.

### Laws and Customs
No grave-robbing (taken very seriously). Weapons are fine; drawing them is not. Salt at the sill, a star at the door, mirrors covered in a house with a death in it, the dead named once and released. Outsiders are tolerated and put to work quickly; you earn trust here by how you treat other people's dead.

### Local Colour
Peat-boys with barrows, netmenders arguing over a torn seine, a fen-guide who will take your coin and then spend an hour telling you why you should not go. A funeral is a whole-town event and no one works through one.

# Kettle Bridge

**Town · ~600 people · the region's main river crossing**

### First Impression
The road lifts onto a great old causeway and crosses the peat-dark Mirewend on a bridge older than the town — three massive Concord arches with a newer timber span patched over a collapsed fourth. The town clusters at both ends: mills turning on the current, a market that never quite closes, the smell of river-mud and frying eel. A toll-chain stretches across the road. Boatmen shout from below.

### Public Overview
Kettle Bridge grew on the toll. Anyone crossing the river overland passes here, and the Compact funds a large share of its budget from the fee. It is a working river-port — peat barges, salt-carts, timber from the eastern forest, pilgrims — governed directly by a resident bailiff. It is the liveliest place in the Reach and the worst place in it to keep a secret.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Toll-Span** — the bridge itself, the chain, and the patched fourth arch.
- **The Eel Market** — the crossroads market, open most hours, cheap and loud.
- **The Wet Ward** — the riverbank quarter of boatmen and bargers under the arches.
- **The Compact House** — the bailiff's seat, the watch barracks, and the lockup.
- **Two Houses Lane** — a Ashen Wardens' rest and a Mourners' house, side by side, sharing a wall and a well.

### Leadership
A resident **bailiff** appointed by the Compact, with a sergeant and six to eight watchmen. Bailiffs here are usually ambitious and usually convinced the posting is beneath them, which shapes local politics more than anyone admits.

### Shops and Services
**The Drowned Kettle** inn (about 6 sp a night, good food, the loudest common room in the region). Toll-house (roughly 2 cp on foot, 5 cp with a cart). A grain-mill, chandlers, a boatwright, and a competent surgeon on the Compact's payroll. Boat passage downriver toward the coast or upriver toward the highland edge can be hired at the wharf.

### Laws and Customs
Strict compact law, firmly enforced: no blades drawn on the span, the toll paid in full, the lockup for brawlers. Custom holds that you drop a coin in the river for a safe crossing, and that you never speak a drowned person's name while standing on the bridge.

### Local Colour
Toll-farmers and barge crews conducting a permanent low-grade feud; a fishmonger who knows everyone's business and will trade it for yours; carters queued at the chain swearing at the fee.

# Saltmargin

**Town · ~500 people · the western salt gateway**

### First Impression
The road climbs out of the fens onto a low salt-grass shelf and the air changes — brine instead of peat. Saltmargin is a sprawl of drying-sheds, salt-pans, and tar-black timber halls crusted white at the eaves. Gulls. The constant grind of the salt-mill. Stacked barrels everywhere, each chalked with a buyer's mark. People here squint, bargain hard, and wear knotted salt-grass charms at the throat.

### Public Overview
Saltmargin sits where the coast's salt-carts meet the fen roads. It refines and sells the household salt the whole region uses — for preserving, for the doorsill, for the rites — and the price it sets is felt in every inland village. The town is prosperous, anxious about its own margins, and thoroughly resented by the places that have to buy from it.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Weigh-House** — the scale-hall where the salt rate is set; the town's true seat of power.
- **The Pans** — tidal salt-pans and drying-sheds; hard, wet, badly paid work.
- **The Knotted Hand** — the town's only inn, named for the salt-grass charm.
- **Brine Chapel** — a small salt-blessed shrine of the Mourners' Circle; every shipment is blessed before it leaves.
- **The Tar Wharf** — where coast carts unload. Things also move here after dark.

### Leadership
No reeve of its own. The compact governs at arm's length through a **warden of weights** — a tired, honest office concerned mainly with fraud at the scale — while the Cinder Ledger's wharf-factor sets actual policy through pricing.

### Shops and Services
Lodging at the Knotted Hand (about 5 sp). The weigh-house sells salt in bulk and offers loans. A ropewalk and cooperage, a charm-maker, a hedge-surgeon who mostly treats pan injuries, carts and a guide for the coast road. No temple healer; the chapel offers folk blessing only.

### Laws and Customs
Compact law applies, lightly. Every shipment is salt-blessed before departure; breaking a salt-barrel is bad luck and a finable offence. Smuggling is an open secret that everyone tolerates so long as the customary cut is paid.

### Local Colour
Pan-crews with cracked hands demanding better wages; a chapel sister blessing barrels in the rain; an inn full of carters comparing what the price was last season.

# Candlewick

**Village · ~300 people · the tidy southern village**

### First Impression
South of the fens the ground firms into real pasture, and Candlewick sits among hedged fields and rush-ponds — whitewashed cob and thatch that actually looks *cared for*. The air smells of tallow and beeswax. The graveyard is the neatest in the region, every stone straight, every plot salted. On the green stands a curiosity: a roofed-over Concord **archive-stone**, half a wall of carved old script that the village has protected for generations without being able to read a word of it.

### Public Overview
Candlewick is the region's small breadbasket and its candle-works, making the rushlights, lamp-oil and funeral tapers used for a hundred miles. It sits on firm ground at the edge of the orchard country and has grown quietly prosperous and quietly smug about it. Lately it draws a steady trickle of scholars from the city who come to study the archive-stone, which the village finds both flattering and unsettling.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Green and Archive-Stone** — the roofed Concord record-wall on the village green.
- **The Tallow Works** — candle- and rushlight-making; the village's livelihood.
- **The Straight Yard** — the famously tidy graveyard.
- **The Beekeep** — apiaries, honey-mead, and the closest thing to an inn.

### Leadership
A **hayward** of the Compact — a prosperous farmer-official who keeps the fields, fences, and peace — with two part-time wardmen. Village opinion carries more weight than the office does.

### Shops and Services
Informal lodging at the beekeeper's table (about 4 sp). The tallow works sells candles, rushlights, lamp-oil, and funeral tapers at bulk rates. A blacksmith and a capable herbalist-midwife. A small chapel of the Three Thresholds with real, if modest, clergy — the most reliable magical healing between Hollowmere and the orchard country. Visiting scholars will sometimes teach the old script to a serious student.

### Laws and Customs
Compact law, gently enforced. Graves kept perfectly straight; funeral tapers bought from the local works and no other; outsiders judged almost entirely on their manners. The archive-stone is village-sacred — defacing it, or so much as talking about selling it, is the great local taboo.

### Local Colour
A chandler-master proud to the point of tedium about his wicks; a grave-keeper who will straighten a stone that is a finger's width off; a scholar on the green tracing carved letters and muttering.

# Greywater Holm

**Village · ~100 people and falling · the last firm ground before the deep fen**

### First Impression
The road gives out into a corduroy of half-sunk logs, and the village rises on a low holm barely above the black water — turf-roofed cottages, a single crooked longhall, and a burial-ground that has crept up onto the only dry rise because the lower graves will not stay dry. Mist hangs even at midday. It is quiet in a way that feels like held breath.

### Public Overview
Greywater Holm was a peat-cutting and fowling village of a couple of hundred souls and is now perhaps half that. Families leave for the towns; the water rises; the fen takes back ground every year. There is no watch and no counting-house here — the Mourners' elder speaks for the village because nobody else will. It is poor, grieving, and stubborn, and it is the last place to buy a guide before the deep fen.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Crooked Longhall** — the only common building. Meetings, meals, and mourning all happen here.
- **The Creeping Ground** — the burial-rise, climbing away from the rising water year by year.
- **The Fen Edge** — duckboards, fowlers' blinds, and a guide's stilt-hut just outside the holm.

### Leadership
An **elder of the Mourners' Circle**, by consent rather than office. Disputes go to her; serious crimes are punished by exile into the fen, which the village regards as sentence enough.

### Shops and Services
No inn — travellers shelter in the longhall by the elder's leave, for a copper or a kindness. One peat-and-fowl trader. No smith, no temple, no magical healing; folk herb-craft only. The village's genuinely valuable export is **fen-guiding**: local guides who know the safe ground, the blinds, and which channels move.

### Laws and Customs
No formal law. Hospitality is real but silent. The dead are buried higher each year and nobody remarks on it. Outsiders are watched warily — but the village badly needs help and will take an offered hand.

### Local Colour
A widow who has moved the same husband's grave-marker twice as the ground shifted; children who can name every safe stepping-log; a fowler who will not go out past the second blind and will not say why.

# Harrowgast

**Mining village · ~400 people · the northern highland**

### First Impression
The road climbs out of the fens into bare grey highland, and Harrowgast appears clinging to a scree slope — slate roofs, spoil-heaps, and the black square mouths of old adits cut into the mountain by hands long dead. Half the town is ruins the living have re-roofed; the other half is ruins nobody will touch. The wind never stops. The folk are lean, soot-marked, and superstitious about the deep tunnels.

### Public Overview
Harrowgast was a Concord mining-delving before the collapse and a salvage town after. Its people work the abandoned upper tunnels for old stone, metal, and "old glass" — a local term for the strange glassy salvage the deep rock yields — which city buyers pay well for. The compact barely reaches here; a single overseer keeps nominal order. It is poor, hard, cold, and lawless at the edges, and it is the only place in the region where the Concord's works stand open instead of drowned.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Living Half** — re-roofed Concord buildings where people actually dwell.
- **The Spoil-Heaps** — where salvage is sorted, and where the buyers keep their weigh-post.
- **The Open Adits** — the worked upper tunnels; ongoing salvage-digging.
- **The Stilled Quarter** — Concord ruins nobody will re-roof. Outside crews camp here anyway.
- **The Deep Mouth** — the barred entrance to the lower workings. Coins are left on the lintel.

### Leadership
An **overseer** of the Compact, in practice a former miner keeping order with his fists and a short rope. Beyond his authority, the miners' own code governs: theft from a dig is the one crime everyone agrees on.

### Shops and Services
**The Cold Drum** — rough tavern and bunkhouse (about 4 sp, watered ale, hot stew, occasional knife-fights). The weigh-post buys salvage and sells tools, rope, lamp-oil and cold-weather gear at a markup. A blacksmith-toolwright. No temple and no healer beyond a bonesetter. Mining and climbing gear here is the best in the region. Guides into the upper adits can be hired, expensively.

### Laws and Customs
Compact law in name; the overseer's word in fact. Never whistle in the deep tunnels. Leave a coin at the Deep Mouth. Outsiders are tolerated if they can work or pay, and disliked if they ask too many questions about how far down the workings go.

### Local Colour
Sorters picking through spoil by lamplight; rival buyers shoving each other at the weigh-post over a crate; a dig-boss losing crews because his men will not go past a certain level and will not explain.

# Reedford

**Hamlet · ~80 people · a river ford and waypoint**

### First Impression
Half a day out of Hollowmere the road dips to a reedy ford where the Mirewend runs wide and shallow over gravel. A dozen cottages, a reed-thatcher's yard, drying-racks of cut reed, and a single shrine-stone on the bank. Children mind geese. A rope-and-plank ferry crosses when the ford floods.

### Public Overview
Reedford cuts and dries the reed that thatches half the region and ferries travellers across the ford. Perhaps eighty people, most of them three or four families. There is no resident authority — a watchman rides through weekly from the nearest town, and a widow of the Mourners' Circle tends the shrine-stone. It is a meal, a bed of straw, and a river crossing, and otherwise keeps entirely to itself.

### Notable Areas
- **The Ford and Ferry** — the shallow crossing and the plank-ferry kept for floods.
- **The Reed-Yard** — drying racks and the thatcher's work.
- **The Bank Stone** — a single old shrine-stone and a tiny burial plot.

### Leadership
None formally. The **thatcher** speaks for the hamlet by force of personality and by being related to most of it.

### Shops and Services
No inn — a meal and straw for a few coppers or a kindness. Reed, thatch, ferry crossing (about 1 cp), and river news. Folk healing only.

### Laws and Customs
Custom and kinship. Disputes go to the thatcher and then, rarely, to the town. A reed is dropped in the river for the dead. The ford is not crossed after dark, "when the water's wrong" — everyone says it, nobody explains it.

### Local Colour
A ferryman who will absolutely not take you across at dusk, at any price, and will not be argued with; geese; a hamlet delighted by any news at all from anywhere.

# The Ashwalk Rest

**Waystation · ~20 residents plus travellers · the great crossroads**

### First Impression
Where four Concord causeways meet on a low rise stands the Rest: a squat, ash-grey hall of old Concord stone, re-roofed and walled with a turf rampart, a star carved deep over the gate and a thin, constant smoke of ritual ash. Travellers shelter in the yard. Inside it is warm, spare, and quiet, smelling of woodsmoke and bitter herbs. After the fens it feels like the first place in days you can put your back to a wall.

### Public Overview
The Ashwalk Rest is the wandering Ashen Wardens' main waystation in the region — not a town but a crossroads sanctuary the order has maintained for centuries. Its brethren base out of it between calls; travellers shelter under its protection; a Mourner shares the hall and tends the road-dead. It charges nothing and accepts labour, food, or donation. It is the safest roof between any two towns in the Reach.

### Notable Areas
- **The Grey Hall** — common hall, hearth, and the order's long table.
- **The Yard and Rampart** — walled ground where carts, travellers, and refugees shelter.
- **The Rite-Room** — where the dead brought in off the roads are laid and tended.
- **The Cellar** — the order's regional records. Not open to visitors.

### Leadership
A **hospitaller** who runs the hall, the stores, and the constant traffic of exhausted brethren. Senior members of the order pass through and take precedence on matters of rite, but the hospitaller runs the house.

### Shops and Services
Sanctuary — safe lodging in hall or yard, free or by donation; the most reliable safe rest in the open country. Rite-aid, lent salt, and a blessing for the road. Folk medicine plus genuine minor healing magic. Basic resupply: salt, herbs, trail food, lamp-oil. No weapons or armour. A warning, and sometimes a guide, before a bad stretch of road.

### Laws and Customs
The house rule is simple: no violence inside the rampart, no trafficking in relics, and the dead come first. Leave the hall as you found it and burn a pinch of ash for the road-dead before you go. All are sheltered regardless of who they are — though the unwelcome sleep in the yard, unarmed and watched.

### Local Colour
Grey-cloaked brethren carried in half-dead from a bad call; families from the fen villages camped by the wall; a long quiet evening at the hearth that is, for most travellers, the best night's sleep in the region.
