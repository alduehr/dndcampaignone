## Summary

Magic in Vael is standard D&D 5e/2024 — arcane, divine, primal, and all the usual classes function normally. The world adds a handful of consistent, lightweight flavor rulings around **Remembrance**, an in-world term for the lingering identity of the dead. These wrinkles mostly affect death, resurrection, and a known category of old relics. They are not a new system — just how magic behaves here.

## Standard Magic

- All standard 5e classes, subclasses, spells, and magic items work normally unless noted below.
- Magic is uncommon but accepted. Hedge-casters, shrine-keepers, and a few trained mages exist in most communities. Casters are not hunted — but death-magic and "calling the dead" carry real social taboo.

## How Magic Behaves Here (Apply Consistently)

These are lightweight, worldbuilding-flavor rulings, not a replacement system — any 5e-compatible game can use them as-is.

1. **Hard resurrection.** *Revivify, Raise Dead, Resurrection,* and similar spells still exist, but each casting is unusually difficult here: treat it as requiring a **DC 12 spellcasting-ability check** (rising with how long the target has been dead) *in addition* to the spell's normal costs. On a failure, the soul does not return clean — a temporary flaw, a missing memory, or (on a bad enough failure) a **wrong-come-back** (see below). Telegraph this danger to the player before they commit to the spell; it should feel like a real, informed risk, not a trap.
2. **Talkative dead.** *Speak with Dead* works unusually well near old ruins and shrines — the dead seem closer than they should be there. Restless dead may even answer briefly without the spell, if approached respectfully.
3. **Wrong-come-back revenants.** A botched resurrection, or a lingering old ruin, can produce a creature that looks like the person who died but is only a partial return: it remembers some things, wants something left unfinished, and isn't fully the same person anymore. Mechanically, flavor a commoner, ghost, specter, or revenant statline as fits the danger — no new stat block is needed. These make for strong, eerie encounters and are a natural source of in-world clues and pathos.
4. **Remembrance relics.** Some old ruins and archives yield relics that seem to hold a "kept memory" — in stone, glass, or bone. Such a relic can replay a fragment of the dead person's memory, power a one-use magical effect, or be "spent" by certain adversaries. Treat these as flavorful plot or utility items; build their exact mechanics to suit whatever story they appear in.
5. **Thin-born.** Occasionally a person is described as "thin-souled" from birth — a real, if poorly understood, phenomenon in this world. By default this carries no mechanical penalty; it's a roleplay and story hook. If a table wants a mechanical expression, keep it to a minor trait rather than a stat penalty.

## Forbidden and Dangerous Magic

- **Calling the dead** (necromantic divination, animating the dead, soul-binding) is socially taboo almost everywhere, and genuinely risky in play — it can draw unwanted attention from whatever lingers at old ruins or shrines. Use this to create escalating complications, not to arbitrarily punish curious casters.
- **Animate Dead / Create Undead** function as normal, but the undead created in this world are unusually "aware" in flavor — unsettling even to allies of a necromancer character. A necromancer PC is fully playable and can be thematically rich here; lean into the moral tension rather than blocking the class.

## Using This Elsewhere

Every ruling above is meant to be usable without reference to any specific plot — "resurrection is hard and risky here," "the dead near old ruins are unusually talkative," "watch out for wrong-come-backs," and "some relics hold a kept memory" are all simply true of how magic behaves in Vael, wherever a story is set. A curious, magically inclined character investigating *why* the world works this way is free to develop their own theory — that discovery belongs to whatever specific campaign they're in.
