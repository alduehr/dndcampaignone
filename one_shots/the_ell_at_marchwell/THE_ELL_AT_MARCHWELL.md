## RUNNING THIS THING — READ FIRST

**Six rules that keep it working:**

1. **Most rooms mean nothing.** The wrong objects, the absurdities, the
   impossible furniture — none of it is a clue. Describe them beautifully and
   never mention them again. If everything means something, the Ell becomes a
   puzzle box and stops being alien.
2. **The horror is arithmetic, not gore.** Nothing chases the party in session
   1. The scariest thing that happens all night is a tape measure.
3. **Every stat block is printed, run as written.** The quirks are cosmetic
   and behavioural and never touch the numbers.
4. **Navigation is theatre-of-mind.** Battle maps for fights only. Do not draw
   the Ell. If a player maps it, let them — the map will be wrong, and that's
   the point, and don't tell them.
5. **Never say "it doesn't work."** Every spell functions. It just functions
   *here*. See **Magic In The Ell** below.
6. **Time is soft.** Nobody in the Ell knows how long they've been in it.
   Track rests, not hours.

**The chain-click.** Every time the space folds, everyone hears a soft double
click — like a measuring chain being taken up. It is never a warning. It is a
receipt. Use it liberally in session 1 before it means anything.

**The fold rule (the thing they must learn):** *identical things are the same
thing.* Two rooms sharing an exact feature — the same crack, the same chipped
knob, the same water stain — are **one location.** Walk through a doorway that
appears twice and you come out of the other one. The person behind you doesn't
follow.

**It is not a gate.** The party can reach the end of this adventure without
ever articulating the fold rule — Hesper walks them to the door in session 4
regardless. It exists to give them agency and to make the finale legible. So
hand it out generously; there is no reason to be stingy with it.

**Seven ways in, no rolls required.** Five of these are things you *do to the
party* on a schedule — none of them waits for a player to have a clever idea.
Only the last two need the party to act, and they're bonuses, not load-bearing.
There's also the door-breaks-the-fold lesson built directly into the Four
Casses fight (see Session 1's climax) — that one teaches itself through
combat, not through description, so it isn't numbered here.

**Push these (they happen whether or not the party cooperates):**

1. **The repeating house** (S1). Five near-identical Marchwell Houses in a
   row, and one of them is one they've already been in. Their own bootprints
   are in the dust, including one mid-stride facing a wall.
2. **Ambry's glove, three times.** He drops it in the first corridor. They
   find it again two rooms later, and a third time an hour after that — and
   it's the same glove, not a copy: torn the same way, still damp. You place
   it; the party never has to think of anything.
3. **The marching order breaks.** The character *at the back* of the line
   walks through a door and comes out at the front, facing the party. Everyone
   watches it happen. No flash, no sound, no sense of transit.
4. **Ambry's tally wall** (S2), found empty and unexplained: a wall counting
   the doors twice by two different routes, arriving at the same number both
   times, the second count gone over so hard the chalk has broken. It makes
   the argument on its own. He confirms it afterwards, at length, whether or
   not anyone asks.
5. **Cut into the plaster in a neat surveyor's hand:**
   > **TWO OF A THING IS ONE OF A THING**

**Bonuses (only if the party tries something):**

6. **Ambry's rule of thumb**, if they question him: *"Don't use a door you've
   seen before."* Wrong, and nearly right.
7. **Hesper says it flat** (S3), if somehow nobody has got there: *"Anything
   the same as another thing *is* that thing. It can't tell them apart. That's
   all this place is."*

### Where do you come out? (the set rule)

**Identical things aren't several things that link — they are one thing with
several mouths.** Five identical doorways are not five doors. They are *one
door* the instrument has filed five times, and it has no way to tell the
copies apart, so neither can anything else.

That gives you a clean, table-fast ruling:

| Identical doorways in the set | What happens when you go through |
|---|---|
| **2** | Deterministic. In one, out the other, every time. A reliable shortcut. |
| **3+** | Random. Roll among the other mouths. Nobody can predict it — **including the Ell**, because there is no difference for it to choose on. |

**So duplication is chaos and uniqueness is control**, which is the whole
lesson of the adventure expressed in one table:

- **Defacing** a doorframe removes it from the set. Cut a mark into three of a
  five-set and you've turned a lottery into a reliable two-way shortcut. This
  is the single most satisfying thing a clever party will do all campaign.
- **Copying** a mark *adds* to a set — useful for deliberately linking two
  distant rooms, dangerous if you copy something that already has cousins.
- **A room the party has made unique is in no set at all**, which is exactly
  why it's rest-safe.

**And it's why Hesper is so hard to pin down.** She hasn't memorised "the
doorways" — she's memorised **which doorways are in two-sets.** She only ever
folds through matched pairs, so her movement is deterministic while everyone
else's is a gamble. A party that notices she always uses the same handful of
doors has found her weakness.

Once they have it, **let them use it.** Scratching a doorframe makes a room
unique. Copying a mark folds two rooms together deliberately. Defacing a mark
strands whatever's following them. This is the single best thing in the
adventure — do not gatekeep it.

## RESTING IN THE ELL

### The rule: you can only long rest in a room the Ell can tell apart

A long rest requires a **unique** room — one the instrument cannot confuse
with any other. Anywhere else, the room folds while they sleep and they wake
scattered across three corridors.

**What a failed long rest actually gives them:** treat it as a completed
*short* rest — they may spend Hit Dice as normal for a short rest, and
nothing else recovers. No exhaustion, no HP loss beyond what they already
had. Add one lost hour finding each other again before play can continue.

A room qualifies if:

- **They've made it unique.** A scratched doorframe, a scored wall, a name cut
  into the plaster. This is the fold rule paying rent, and it is the answer
  90% of the time — *"did you mark it?"* resolves almost every case.
- **It contains something one-of-a-kind.** The anchor. The gallows. The well.
  The milestone. The wrong objects are the safe houses of the Ell, and the
  party will work this out and start *seeking them out*, which is delightful
  and costs you nothing to run.
- **Somebody already made it unique.** Ambry's squat qualifies. So does every
  camp Hesper has ever kept, and she knows where all of them are — which is a
  large part of why having her along feels so good in session 3.

Marking a room takes ten minutes and a piece of chalk is not enough; it has to
be **cut**, because chalk gets copied. A dagger, a chisel, a nail. Somebody
will realise this the first time they wake up in three pieces.

**Do not make this a gotcha.** Warn them once, up front — see *Table ruling*
below — and then let the rule bite honestly.

### Safety valve: if they fail two long rests, show them the answer

A party that never cracks the fold rule cannot reliably make a safe room, and
will grind down on attrition through the two hardest fights in the campaign.
That is the **one place in this adventure where a missed inference becomes a
real punishment**, and you should not let it happen.

**After the second failed long rest, they find somebody else's camp.**

A small room with a bed frame, a burnt-down candle, a tin cup, and a folded
blanket. Cut deep into the doorframe with a knife, in a neat surveyor's hand:
a name, a date a hundred years old, and a single scored line beneath it. The
room is *obviously* safe and it is obviously safe *because somebody cut a mark
into it.*

Let them sleep there. Do not explain it. **The physical evidence makes the
argument** — and it plants Hesper a full session before she walks in, so her
arrival lands as *"oh — it's her"* rather than as a new NPC.

If they somehow still don't connect it, **Ambry does**, once he's with them:
he never worked out *why*, but three weeks of trial and error taught him
*"sleep where somebody's cut something, or near the big strange objects."*
That's enough to function on, and it's the shape of the truth without the
reason — which is very much his character.

### The cost: they get a good reading

Rests always work when the room qualifies — full HP, full slots, no attrition,
no exhaustion rules. **But eight hours of lying still is the best measurement
the instrument has ever had of them**, and it takes it.

| Long rest | What turns up later |
|---|---|
| **1st** | A copy of something they own, in a room ahead of them. Not a similar object — *theirs*, down to the repair. They will check their pack. It's still in their pack. |
| **2nd** | Something speaks in the voice of whoever slept deepest. Not words they said. Words they *would* say. |
| **3rd** | A copy of a party member, in a room, going about a routine — sharpening, cooking, mending — calmly, competently, not hostile, and not interested in them. It does not fight and cannot be talked to. If they kill it, it dies easily and there's nothing inside it. |
| **4th+** | More of the same, in combination, and by now they should be afraid to sleep — which costs them nothing mechanically and everything else. |

**Ambry, if he's with them, notices the pattern before they do**, because it
happened to him for three weeks and nobody was there to tell.

### The rests are the finale's difficulty dial

**Count every long rest they take. That number is how many copies of the party
stand behind Hesper in session 4.**

This is the whole point of the cost, and it is the best kind of difficulty
setting: **the players choose it themselves, in advance, without being told
they're choosing it.** A cautious party that rests after every fight walks
into the last room and finds five of themselves waiting. A party that pushed
through on half resources arrives exhausted to a fair fight.

Copies use the **doppelganger** stat block (CR 3), same as the Four Casses,
and they are *the party* — same faces, same gear, same posture, silent. They
fight competently and without any flourish. **Cap at 5, or the party-size cap
in the scaling table below, whichever is lower**; past that, add HP to Hesper
instead. See session 4.

Neither outcome is a punishment. Both are earned, and the party will
understand exactly why the moment they see the room.

### Table ruling: don't tell them which rest they're getting

**Say this once, out loud, the first time anyone proposes a rest, and then
never again:**

> "I'm not going to tell you in advance whether a rest here will be a long one
> or a short one. It isn't random — it depends on something — and there may be
> a cost either way. Decide accordingly."

That's the whole ruling. It's honest, it hands them a real problem, and it
converts every rest from bookkeeping into a wager. The uncertainty **resolves
into mastery**: the moment they crack what makes a room unique, they can
predict it perfectly and the wager becomes a plan. Which is the arc the entire
adventure is built on.

*(This replaces the "telegraph it once by letting them wake up scattered"
approach — you're front-loading the warning instead of demonstrating it, which
is cleaner and costs the party nothing they weren't warned about.)*

## MAGIC IN THE ELL

| Spell | What happens |
|---|---|
| *Misty step* / *dimension door* | Works perfectly, and obeys the fold rule. Sometimes you arrive somewhere identical, and elsewhere. |
| *Locate object* | Points in nine directions at once. There are nine copies. |
| *Sending* | Reaches something. It answers, in the sender's own voice, with the sender's own phrasing. (After session 2, this is the oblex.) |
| *Speak with dead* | Accurate, courteous answers about a house the corpse has never seen. |
| *Detect magic* | One continuous, seamless aura across everything, including the floor. Conjuration — "the way a map is conjuration." |
| *Augury* / *divination* | Always weal **and** woe. Always. |
| *Dispel magic* | Nothing to dispel. The equipment isn't a spell; it's measurement, not magic. |
| *Passwall* / tunnelling | Works. Behind the plaster is more plaster. Behind that, at depth 4+, is notation. |
| *Teleport* / *word of recall* | Out of level range, but if it comes up: arrives at the correct destination, which is a drafted copy of it. |
| *Light* / *darkvision* | Fine at depth 1. From depth 2 the tallow light overrides both — no shadows to see by, nothing to illuminate. |

## THE REVELATION LADDER — VEYR, THE MEASURE, AND THE ECHO

Two questions run under the whole back half of this adventure: **"Where are
we?"** and **"What is the Ell?"** Answer them in stages across Sessions 3–4,
never all at once, and never mainly through a note the party reads. The
strongest deliveries here are things the party watches happen, touches, or
fights — not paragraphs of lore. This section coordinates beats that are
written out in full at their own place in the text; use it to keep the
sequence straight, not as a substitute for reading those scenes.

**Two kinds of evidence, and let the contrast do the work:** everything
pointing at **the Measure** (the mortal order that staked out this region of
the Echo and left equipment behind) looks like tools — survey chains,
notation, tallies, calibration, drafting. Everything pointing at **Veyr and
the Echo** (the domain the Measure surveyed, not one they built) looks like
presence — a voice, a mark cut twice, something watching that was never
mortal. The party should be able to sort evidence into those two piles well
before anyone tells them there are two piles.

| Stage | Where | What lands | What stays open |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (mandatory) | S2 midpoint, the oblex's confidence and its death-line ("Veyr remembers you") | Something ancient, native, and *interested* is down here — not lost, not the Ell itself. | Whose voice? What's "Veyr"? |
| 2 (mandatory) | S2 end, the false house destabilising + a distant, inhuman screech | Depth 3 is inhabited by something that isn't the equipment. | What is it? |
| 3 (mandatory) | S3, the first follower, placed in a corridor between any two of the twelve Depth 3 rooms so it can't be skipped | They're being watched and catalogued specifically, not just recorded as part of a room. | Why? By whom? |
| 4 (optional) | S3, the second and third followers, only if the party escalates | Killing the observer doesn't stop the observation. | Same, sharper. |
| 5 (optional) | S3, the Cutter's Rest (Room 12 of the Depth 3 bank) | A name, stamped into a surveyor's own tool: **"the Measure."** Someone built this on purpose, a long time ago, and worshipped something while they did it. | Who were they? What did they want? What's the mark they carved? |
| 6 (optional) | S3, the real Cass rescue | Drafts are loyal to what was actually, repeatedly measured — not to a whole person. | Doesn't yet say who's doing the measuring or why. |
| 7 (mandatory once she joins) | S3 end, Hesper | Confirms someone ancient did this on purpose — her own folk-guess, **"the chain-crew."** | She has never learned it has a name, a god, or a reason. Say so if asked; it's true, not evasive. |
| 8 (mandatory) | S4 opening, the substrate's Ashgarden Vale recognition (see Change #16, below) | Scale: the Ell has measured far more than one house — the whole vale, obsessively, more than once. | Still doesn't say who, or why. |
| 9 (mandatory) | S4, the reconstruction station (see Change #17, below), a piece of the Measure's own equipment built up against a half-buried carved shape bearing the same doubled mark from the Cutter's Rest and the oblex's token | Concrete mechanism: the equipment measures *behavior*, not just dimension — and mortal-built machinery and something never built by mortals occupy the same space, physically, right there. **If the party has the Twice-Cut Token, this is also where the mark itself gets explained** (not named): held against the wall-carving, it reads as the fold rule drawn as a picture of itself. | Confirms two separate things exist here: mortal equipment, and the older, uncreated domain it was built inside — and gives the closest thing to an answer for what that domain's power actually *is*, if not what it's called. |
| 10 (backstop) | S4, Hesper's own summary, if the party still hasn't pieced it together | Ties stages 1–9 together in her own words — without ever naming Veyr or the Measure, because she genuinely doesn't know them. | The actual names stay a research hook for after the adventure, not a requirement to finish it. |

**Nothing here is required to escape.** A party that never finds the Cutter's
Rest, never rescues Cass, and never presses Hesper still gets stages 1, 2, 3,
7, 8, and 9 automatically — that's enough to know *something* built this on
purpose and something else already lived where they built it, which is the
whole point. The names "Veyr" and "the Measure" are deliberately rare and
precious; most tables will leave this adventure with the shape of the truth
and not the words for it, and that's the correct outcome, not a failure
state.

## SESSION 1 — IN

*Depths 0–1. No combat. **4–5 hrs.***

### The chase (15–20 min — play this, don't summarize it)

The party has a writ for **Ferris Ambry**, under-clerk, 41, who has spent
three weeks quietly bleeding the tithe office dry: a little from the first
parish, more from the second, and — this morning — the rest of it, in a
single panicked grab, from the third. About 800gp all told, in coin and
chapel plate, on a borrowed pony that is not built for this.

**Open here, not at the farm gate.** The party rides into the third parish an
hour behind him, still thinking this is a paperwork job. The sexton confirms
it: yes, a nervous little clerk was through not long ago, badly out of
breath, asking about the road west. And there — tied at the lych-gate,
blown, foundering, one shoe half off — is **Ambry's pony**, which he has
finally ridden into the ground and abandoned.

**Let them question somebody** — the sexton, a verger, a parishioner
sweeping the step — about which way he went on foot. Insight or a plain
Wisdom (Perception) check (**DC 12**) notices the stable door standing ajar
that shouldn't be, a beat before anyone finishes the sentence.

**Then the racket.** A shout from the stable-yard, a horse screaming, a rail
going over. Ambry — sobbing, apologising to nobody, still clutching the
strongbox under one arm — is dragging himself onto a fresh horse he has no
business touching. **He's off and riding before anyone can close the
distance.** This is not a fight; it's a **mounted chase**, and it should
feel like one: a Dexterity (Animal Handling) or group check (**DC 12**, one
failure is fine, two means he opens a real lead) to stay with him across
broken parish ground, hedgerows, and a flooded cart-track, while he does
everything wrong and stays ahead anyway because panic is a better motivator
than skill.

**He doesn't get away — he gets somewhere.** The chase ends when he cuts
through a gap in a hedge onto the west road and the party sees exactly where
he's making for: a whitewashed farmhouse at the end of a long lane, orchard
smoke rising behind it. **Let a player who asks around learn it first, if
they think to ask before they ride:** that's Marchwell land, and the clerk
has a cousin there.

**Marchwell House.** Cider-orchard country, half a day west of Tilbrook.
Whitewashed, four rooms down and three up, a press-house, a kitchen garden, an
ash-house. Autumn; the pressing is on; the yard smells of apples going over.

**Timing:** the chase buys Ambry roughly two hours alone at the farm before
the party arrives — long enough to believe, wrongly, that he has shaken
them; not long enough to have done anything but eat, hide the box, and start
to relax. Everything in "The search," below, is built on that two hours.

**The cast:**

- **Oda Marchwell** (50s, widow, runs the orchard). Lies well and lies
  immediately: no, they've had no one. Her tell is that she's *too* relaxed
  about a party of six armed strangers at her gate. Insight DC 13 catches it.
  She is hiding two things and the party will assume it's one. **She has no
  family lore to fall back on and no idea what she's actually managing** —
  the pantry has only been wrong since last midsummer, so everything she's
  doing about it (the chalk jar, never sending Tobin for stores, never
  saying the word "pantry" more than she has to) is something she worked
  out herself, this past year, alone. Play her as improvising, not as a
  keeper of old family knowledge.
- **Cass** (her brother, 60s). Sleeps in the kitchen chair. Drinks. Quiet,
  courteous, agreeable — agrees with whatever the last person said. **Hasn't
  gone upstairs in a month.**

  **DM-only: the thing in the chair is not Cass.** The real Cass went into the
  Ell a month ago and never came out; the instrument put a draft of him back in
  the house, because a resident who opens doors brings it more to measure. It
  is not malicious and it is barely a person. It does one thing on purpose in
  its whole existence, and it does it in the next ten minutes.

  **It doesn't go upstairs because it has never been upstairs.** The draft was
  taken from a man who was measured downstairs. There is nothing of the first
  floor in it, and it cannot walk into a room it doesn't contain. Nobody needs
  to work this out — it simply pays off in one line later, and the players will
  remember that the file told them in the first five minutes.
- **Tobin** (17, her son). Polite, affectionate, helping at the press. **He
  counts things.** Apples into the barrow. Steps across the yard. The party's
  horses. The party. He does it under his breath, constantly, and he isn't
  aware of it. He has stopped eating much. He went through the door in
  midsummer with a lantern and a ball of twine and came back four days later
  unhurt and hungry, and nobody in that kitchen has asked a hard question
  since.
  *He is atmosphere. Nothing hinges on him. Do not build a mystery here.*

### The door closes (minute one — and then nothing happens for an hour)

**When the last character steps into the house, Cass shuts the front door
behind them.** Unhurried, both hands, like a man shutting out a draught.

**Oda sees him do it.** She says *"No—"*, one syllable, and she is too late,
and then she stops, and her face closes, and she offers them tea.

*Play it as nothing.* An old man shut a door. If a player asks, Cass says
it was letting the cold in. Oda changes the subject and she is very good at
it. Insight DC 15 gets: *she is now frightened of something that is not you,
and she has decided not to say so.*

**Every door out of the house is now a way into the Ell.** The party will not
find out for forty-five minutes.

### The search (and the discovery)

**Ambry has had about two hours here** — long enough to relax, eat, hide the
box, and genuinely believe he'd shaken them. **He was partway through dinner
with the family when the party actually arrived at the house** — that's
what breaks it. Everything below should read as the ordinary debris of a
frightened man's two hours, not as a set of puzzle-clues waiting to be
decoded; let them find these however they search, and don't make anyone
roll to notice the ones marked guaranteed.

**Guaranteed — they will find this without trying:**

- **His coat**, still warm, thrown over the back of a kitchen chair — a
  clerk's good coat, out of place on a farm, with a tithe-office button on
  the cuff, the collar dark with dried sweat. He just shed it the moment he
  got inside; no disguise, no borrowed clothes, just a hot, exhausted man
  taking his coat off.

**Salt the rest around the house — pick three or four, not all of them, so no
single miss can stall the scene:**

| Clue | Where | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| A third bowl at the table, spoon still in it, going cold | Kitchen | An extra place, abandoned mid-bite, very recently — someone was eating with the family and left without finishing. |
| Fresh, city-soled bootprints | Pantry flour, leading to the door | Not a farm boot. Fresh. Only going one way. |
| A cup drained fast, foam still clinging to the sides | Kitchen | Someone was desperately thirsty, recently. |
| A second horse's tack, unaccounted for | Stable | Nobody at Marchwell owns a horse that fits this saddle. |
| A tithe-office ledger page, torn and stuffed behind a jar | Pantry shelf | Names, sums, and the vale's tithe seal — undeniably his. |

**Where the box actually is:** with him. He never let go of it long enough to
hide it — a drag-mark of a heavy, square-cornered box scrapes across the
pantry's flour dust, dead straight, ending at the door, and Oda will admit,
if pressed, that she watched him wrestle it in there himself, muttering that
he'd "sort it once things had quieted." **It goes into the Ell with him**,
and the party finds it again, abandoned, once it got too heavy for a man who
hasn't stopped running — see "Depth 1 — the repeating house," below, and
**The prisoner and the silver**.

Let them corner Oda. Let them get comfortable.

**Then somebody goes to leave.**

The front door — the one they came in by, an hour ago, in daylight — opens
onto a hundred and forty feet of corridor with a rag rug in it.

Every exterior door in the house does the same. The back door. The
press-house door. All of them, corridor.

**Then the windows.** And the windows are the worst thing in the session.

Through the glass, everything is **completely normal.** The orchard. The
yard. Their own horses at the rail. Sun, cloud shadow, a hedge moving in the
wind. Anyone who looks out of a window sees an ordinary autumn afternoon and
will say so.

**Open one, and it stops.**

Not gradually. The moment the sash lifts, the wind quits mid-gust. The birds
are gone — not flown, *gone*, and nobody can say when. The leaves settle and
hold. The horses stand exactly as they were and do not shift their weight, do
not swish, do not breathe. The light is the right colour and comes from
nowhere. It smells of nothing at all.

Close the window and the wind comes back through the glass.

Let them do this six or seven times. Let a different player try it. Let
somebody hold the window open for a full minute and describe, in detail, one
still leaf.

**They can climb out.** They should — it's the obvious move and denying it is
cheap. Outside is an orchard: silent, birdless, windless, under a sky that
does not move, and the grass takes a footprint and keeps it. Walk away from
the house in a straight line for twenty minutes and the house is in front of
them again. Round the corner to the front and the horses are there, patient
and warm and not breathing.

**That's the trap, and it is airtight, and it took four sentences.**

**DM-only:** the moment Cass shut the front door, the house they are standing
in stopped being the house. It's a draft — a good one, the instrument's best
work, because it's the thing it's been copying most recently. The real
Marchwell House is out in the real sun with nobody in it and the door standing
open. The still orchard outside the windows is the same draft, and it is the
party's first sight of depth 1 without knowing that's what it is.

**And Cass gets up and walks into the Ell.** Not fleeing — he opens the back
door, steps into the corridor, and pulls it to behind him, the same unhurried
way he shut the front one. Oda screams her brother's name at a closed door.

**Oda and Tobin are trapped with them**, and they are real, and they are no
use whatsoever. Oda will not go into the corridor and will not stop asking the
party to bring her brother back. Tobin sits on the stairs and counts.

**The only way anywhere is the corridor.** They don't choose to go in — they
run out of every other option, one window at a time, over about twenty
minutes. Which is exactly how it should feel.

### The pantry (the set-piece)

**The pantry has no door to the outside. It never did.**

It's a proper pantry, full-sized, the kind any working farmhouse has —
shelves of preserves down both long walls, a flour bin, a hanging ham, hooks
for tools, the height-notches of three generations of Marchwell children on
the door-jamb, the last one Tobin's at fourteen. One door in, off the
kitchen. That's all it has ever been.

**Open the door and the wrongness needs no trick to find.** The near half of
the room is exactly what it should be — shelves, the flour bin, the ham.
Past that, where the back wall belongs, the shelving simply stops and the
room keeps going: a hundred and forty feet of bare corridor with a rag rug
in it, visible from the doorway before anyone sets foot inside. Nobody has
to open a hole in anything or step through to find out. They can just look.

**Walk the kitchen garden outside and check.** The exterior wall behind
where the pantry sits is whitewashed brick, unbroken, fourteen inches thick,
with a single hopper-window up near the eave — too small for anything but a
cat, and looking, from outside, into an ordinary pantry exactly the depth
the house has room for. There is nothing wrong with this wall. There never
was. **The pantry the party is standing in and the pantry visible from the
garden are the same size. The corridor simply isn't in either of them.**

**Let them test it as many ways as they want — it never resolves:**

- **Rope it.** Tie a line to the door-jamb, walk it down the corridor as far
  as anyone likes, then have someone outside in the real kitchen garden try
  to find where that rope ought to surface — dig at the wall, tap along it,
  anything. **There's nothing to find.** The corridor doesn't run behind,
  above, or under anything the house has. It isn't spatially connected to
  the building it appears to open from.
- **Sound doesn't cross at all.** A character eighty feet down the corridor,
  shouting, is inaudible from the kitchen garden with an ear pressed to the
  exact spot the pantry occupies. There is no thin, far-off version of this
  the way the front door's corridor at least pretends to relate to the
  orchard. This one doesn't pretend.
- Let different people check from both sides, more than once. **It never
  once resolves.** This isn't a corridor folded cleverly around a real
  house. It's a room that has stopped ending where a room like this always
  ended, in plain view, the moment anyone opens the door.

**DM-only, and it matters for the timeline:** this is the same door Tobin
went through last midsummer, and it has been quietly live ever since — the
one door in the house Oda has not treated as ordinary since he came back.
That's why the chalk and twine live on its shelf, and why she keeps finding
reasons to fetch stores herself rather than send anyone else in. **It
doesn't need Cass to open it the way the front, back, and press-house doors
do** — those the instrument claims fresh, from a resident, when the party
arrives. The pantry was claimed months ago and never gave itself back.

**Ambry didn't flee into it, and didn't know what it was.** He genuinely
believed he'd shaken the party — he was two hours ahead of them, fed,
sitting down to dinner with the family, relaxed for the first time all day.
What broke it was the party actually arriving at the house, not the sound of
hooves on the lane (he'd already ignored two or three of those that
afternoon, farm traffic being farm traffic). Panicking at the real thing —
voices, a knock, Oda's face changing — he bolted for the nearest door away
from the kitchen: the pantry, meaning only to hide among the shelves until
the strangers left. **He had no idea it had stopped being a dead end
months ago.** He pulled the door shut behind him and pressed back into what
he still believed was six feet of shelving — and by the time his groping
hand found no back wall, going forward was easier than going back through a
door with the party's voices right behind it. He kept retreating, telling
himself he'd double back once they'd gone, and by the time he admitted there
was no wall to reach, he no longer knew which direction the door was.

Oda's second lie collapses here. She'll say: *"He went out the back."* — and
in the most dangerous possible sense, through the one door in the house that
was never going to lead outside at all, that's exactly true.

### The notebook (do not skip this)

On the pantry shelf beside the door, where anyone searching the room finds
them without a check: **a jam jar packed with chalk stubs**, a ball of twine
with the end cut off short, and a small, cheap, water-warped notebook — all
of it Tobin's own kit, left exactly where he dropped it the day he came back,
because nobody in this house has been able to bring themselves to move it.

**Read the notebook out loud, or hand over a printed copy — it works either
way, but it lands harder read aloud, slowly.**

The first few pages are a seventeen-year-old being sensible about a strange
errand:

> **MARK EVERY DOOR.**
> **COUNT YOUR TURNS.**
> **MIND THE TIME.**

Underneath, a working method — small sketches of doorframes, a route in his
own shorthand, turn counts, step counts, tallied and re-totalled, careful and
almost dull.

**Then it changes, and you should let the pages do the work rather than
narrating the change:**

- The routing gets harder to follow. A count is crossed out and restarted.
  Then restarted again, pressed hard enough to tear the paper.
- **COUNT YOUR TURNS** starts turning up in the margins, then between lines,
  written smaller and smaller as if there wasn't room for it, though there
  plainly was.
- Whole pages, later, are nothing but **COUNT YOUR TURNS** written over and
  over, and then — later still — just **COUNT**, filling every margin,
  sideways up both edges, crammed into corners, a boy's handwriting getting
  faster and less like handwriting.

**The last page is nearly empty.** No route, no count, no method. Just one
line, dead centre, in handwriting far steadier than the pages before it:

> **TRUST NO ONE.**

**It is not written in anything Tobin came back with.** Not the chalk in the
jar, not charcoal, not pencil, not ink from any bottle in this house. Nobody
can identify the material, and nothing about it should be explained yet —
not who wrote it, not when, not what it means. Let the table sit with it.

**This does everything the old note used to do, and more.** Six adventurers
handed a boy's notebook that says *MARK EVERY DOOR, COUNT YOUR TURNS* will
mark every door in the Ell unprompted, which is exactly what you want — and
now they're doing it holding the exact object that shows them, in slow
motion, what happens to a mind that keeps doing it for four straight days.

**And Tobin's marks are already in there** — a different hand, smaller and
neater than the party's, on hundreds of doorframes, copied and recopied until
they're everywhere and mean nothing. That's the lesson delivered *before* the
party makes a mark of their own: **marking naively doesn't work.** They'll
improve on it. That improvement is the fold rule.

*(The notebook's escalating counting compulsion, and the unidentified final
line, are not solved here. Both pay off later — see Change #12's revelation
ladder in "Session 3 — Deeper" and "Session 4 — Out," below; "TRUST NO ONE"
in particular is never explicitly resolved to one single author, and that's
deliberate — see the "Who wrote it" note in Session 4, Scene 2.)*

**On the Ell side of the doorframe: the tally.**

*Where they see it:* the moment the first character steps into a corridor and
looks back at the door they just came through — which is the first thing
anyone does in a strange hallway. **It is on the inside frame of every door
out of the house**, front, back, pantry, press-house, and it is **the same
tally on all of them**, identical mark for identical mark. (Of course it is.
Identical things are the same thing. Nobody needs to notice this in session 1,
and one player will, and they will be right for the wrong reason.)

If nobody looks back, have a fresh scratch **cut itself into the wood** as the
last character crosses — a small dry sound, like a fingernail on a matchbox,
right beside their head.

Hundreds of small scratches, oldest at the top, going back further than the
paint. **Most of them are struck through.** A few are not.

The rule (DM-only, never stated): **a scratch is cut when someone crosses in,
and struck through when they come back out.** It is bookkeeping. It is the
instrument counting stock, and it is completely honest.

At the moment the party first sees it:

| Scratch | Who | Struck through? |
|---|---|---|
| Midsummer | Tobin | **Yes** — he walked out |
| A month ago | Cass | No |
| An hour ago | Ambry | No |
| Just now, as each crosses | The six of them | No |

So: **one month-old scratch, and seven fresh ones, all open.** Let a player
count them. Don't explain anything. *(Payoff: session 4.)*

**Note that draft-Cass has no mark at all** — nothing the Ell made is ever on
the list. That's Hesper's entire problem, and the party has been looking at
the proof since the first hour.

### Depth 1 — the repeating house (rest of the session)

A hundred and forty feet of corridor with a rag rug in it. Then a door. Then
**Marchwell House.**

Same kitchen. Same rag rug. Same scorch on the same hearthstone. Nobody home.
The fire is lit and gives no heat. The light through the windows is an hour
that doesn't match the one they left. The orchard beyond the glass is
perfectly still — no wind, no birds, ever, and it stays that way however long
anyone watches.

**The checkable tells:** the faces in the framed samplers aren't finished. The
food in the pantry is real and has no taste. The height-notches on the
doorframe stop partway up — the copy was taken before the last few were cut.

Going out the front door of this house puts them in a corridor. **The only way
on is the pantry.**

**It recurses, degrading:**

| Copy | What's wrong |
|---|---|
| 2nd | Nearly perfect. One wallpaper seam doesn't match. |
| 3rd | One wallpaper pattern instead of six. Books have covers, no words. |
| 4th | Rooms the right shape, the wrong things in them. Cass's chair is in the pantry. |
| 5th | Furniture is *generic* — a chair, not that chair. Nothing is anyone's. |
| 6th+ | Not a house any more. Rooms. Depth 2 has started and nobody announced it. |

**Ambry's trail** through all of it: a dropped glove, a torn ledger page, then
a **strongbox** sitting open in a corridor, its compact set of chapel plate
still in it, abandoned because it got heavy. That detail tells the party
exactly how long he's been down here, and it's a lie — from their side he's
been in for four hours.

### SESSION 1 CLIMAX — The Four Casses

They have been following him all session. In the fifth copy — the one where
the furniture has gone generic — they catch up.

He is sitting in a chair that is nearly his chair, in a kitchen that is nearly
the kitchen, with his back to them. He says, without turning round and without
any particular feeling, *"You shouldn't have come in."*

**Then the door behind them opens and Cass walks in.** And the pantry door
opens and Cass walks in. And Cass stands up out of the chair.

**Four of him.** Identical — same cardigan, same broken thumbnail, same
unhurried way of closing a door. Because the instrument measured a thing, and
what it does with a thing it has measured is *make more of them.*

**Four doppelgangers** (CR 3), printed stat block, with one quirk: **they do
not shapeshift.** They are stuck as Cass and always will be. Read Thoughts and
Surprise Attack work as written, and Surprise Attack should land — the party
walked in expecting one frightened old man.

They fight in near-silence, apologetically, and they **use the doorways**:
every round, one of them steps through a door and comes out of a different
identical door across the room. This is the fold rule demonstrated at knifepoint
and the party will absolutely clock it.

**And this fight is where they learn the other half of the rule: chalk gets
copied; damage doesn't.** A mark drawn on one of these four doors is copied to
the other three along with everything else in the room — worthless here, same
as everywhere. Physically wrecking a door is a different kind of change, and
the fold can't survive it.

- **If a player gouges, splits, or breaks one of the four doorframes**
  mid-fight — a bad miss that hits the frame instead, a shove into it, a
  deliberate strike — **show them the result immediately.** The next Cass
  that tries to use that doorway simply can't: it fumbles the fold, stumbles
  through as an ordinary door instead, and loses its turn doing it. That
  doorway has left the set. There are three left, and the fight gets
  visibly, mechanically easier from that point on.
- **If nobody does this on their own, force the moment at the top of round
  3.** One of the Casses — mid-swing, mid-shove, missing an attack, colliding
  with the frame retreating from a bad exchange — puts a door out of true.
  Splintered wood, a bent hinge, whatever fits the fiction. **Immediately
  afterward**, have a different Cass step toward that same door to fold and
  simply fail to, faltering in the doorway a half-second too long — an
  opening, if anyone's paying attention.
- **One line, only if the table needs it, never more:** a Cass registers the
  broken frame with something almost like surprise — *"That one's
  different now,"* or *"You spoiled it."* Don't explain the rule in
  dialogue beyond that. Let the fight demonstrate it and let them work out
  the rest.

*Scaling: 3 PCs → 2. 4 → 3. 5 → 3 + Surprise Attack. 6 → 4.*

**Encounter math:** 4 × CR 3 = 2,200 XP, ×2 for numbers = 4,400 adjusted
against a hard threshold of 4,400 for six level-5s. A proper fight to end on,
with no wipe risk.

**End the session** when the last one goes down — and it goes down without a
sound, and there's no blood in it, and it is not quite a body. And somewhere
behind them, the chain-click.

*(The real Cass is alive at depth 4. See session 3.)*

## SESSION 2 — DOWN

*Depth 2. **4–5 hrs.** Ends at level 6.*

**The tallow light** starts here: flat, sourceless, no shadows in it. Torches
become pointless and nobody can say quite when. **The hum** starts — a held
note on a wet glass rim, constant, and players will stop hearing it and then
notice they've stopped.

**Signature of the depth: the wrong object.** Every room is a correct,
plausible, well-kept domestic room containing exactly one thing that has no
business in a house — placed carefully, dusted, treated as furniture. Roll or
pick from [`ROOM_TABLES.md`](ROOM_TABLES.md). **They are never clues.**

**The chalk.** The party's own arrows start appearing in corridors ahead of
them, in their own handwriting. If they never marked anything, they find
arrows anyway — in the handwriting of whoever in the party writes most.

### Beats, in order

1. **A wrong-object room or three.** Establish the register. Let them search.
2. **The Coat Closet** (below). First real fight.
3. **Ambry's squat — empty.** Somebody has been living here a long time. A
   nest of curtains and coats. Sixty empty preserve jars, stacked by size,
   the fruit in the remaining ones perfect and not a fruit that grows. A
   ledger with the accounts torn out and days scratched on the flyleaf —
   **twenty-two of them.** And **the tally wall**: an entire wall chalked
   with a count of the doors, done twice by two different routes, arriving at
   the same number both times, with the second count gone over and over so
   hard the chalk has broken. Nobody's home, and the nest is still warm.

   **And on top of the coats: a single glove.** The pair to the one they have
   now found three times in three different corridors — same make, same
   stitching, other hand. That confirms two things at once and neither needs a
   check: **the man is real and they're on his trail**, and the glove they
   keep finding is *one glove*, which means those were not three corridors.

   **THE FIGHT — what drove him out.** There are five doors out of the squat.
   **Three of them are mimics** — the sound-copying kind. That's why the room
   is warm and empty, and why a man who lived here three weeks isn't in it.

   They don't stir while the party searches. They wait. Then one *calls* — a
   voice from the far side of a door, muffled and urgent, saying a name.
   Whose name is your pick: a PC's, or Oda's, or best of all **Ambry's own**,
   since that's the voice they've been hearing in corridors since session 1.

   **Three mimics** (CR 2), printed stat block. They are doors until touched or
   until somebody commits to one. The other two doors are just doors and the
   party has no way to tell — which makes the search tense in hindsight, since
   somebody almost certainly opened a real one first and got away with it.

   *Scaling: 3 PCs → 2. 4–5 → 3. 6 → 3, or 4 if the table is fresh.*

   **And it explains the whole room.** Ambry sat in here for weeks listening to
   a voice on the other side of a door calling him by name. He held out a long
   time — that's what the tally wall and the broken chalk are. Then one night
   he believed it, opened the door, and ran toward whatever was calling.

   That's how he ended up where they find him. **And the party is about to make
   the same walk, on purpose.**

   **Loot here:** the Honest Weight, Ambry's own — see
   [`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md).
4. **The oblex, and Ambry.** The midpoint.
5. **The way home.** See below. Run it immediately, while they're still
   counting their HP.

*(Change #7: the old Scene 3, "the same water stain, twice," is cut — by
this point the party has already lived through the repeating house and the
Four Casses' door-breaks-the-fold lesson, and repeating that beat here added
nothing new. Nothing replaces it; the recovered runtime goes toward the

material.)*

### The Coat Closet

A closet off a landing, far deeper than the landing has room for, hung with
good coats — brushed, shouldered, in the styles of a great many decades. The
party will search them, because players always search coats. The pockets have
things: a ferry token, a child's tooth, a folded bill of sale, a ring, a
letter that stops mid-word.

**Three come off the hooks first.** They're empty. They move anyway, and they
move like there's someone in them.

**A fourth, heavier and older, joins two rounds later** — it takes that long
to work itself off its hook, so the party gets to watch it coming. All four
are **rugs of smothering** (CR 2), run exactly as printed. The rest of the
coats never move at all.

*Scaling: 3 PCs → 2 coats. 4 → 3. 5 → 3 + the heavy one. 6 → 4.*

**Loot here:** the Ferryman's Token and the coat-pocket miscellany — see
[`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md).

### THE MIDPOINT — The Thing That Copies The Marks

A long room, well-lit by nothing, with **people standing about in it at
conversational distances.** Perhaps a dozen. They are slightly wrong copies of
people who came in through doors like this one over the last hundred years —
a bargeman, a girl in a confirmation dress, two men in identical coats, a
surveyor who is *not* Hesper.

They turn. They greet the party **courteously, in the party's own voices,
using the party's own phrasings**, because it has been sampling them for a
session and a half and practising.

> "That's our own bootprint," says the bargeman, in the fighter's voice,
> almost fondly. "Don't use a door you've seen before," says the girl, in
> Ambry's.

It is the only thing in the Ell that is *interested* in them — and it is the
first solid proof, though the party has no way to know it yet, that the Ell
is not the only intelligence down here. **This is not a creature that
wandered in and got stuck.** It belongs here, the way a gull belongs at sea:
native to the Echo itself, the domain this plot was staked out of, older
than Marchwell House, patient in a way nothing built by mortals manages to
be. Do
not explain any of this to the players yet — see Change #12's revelation
ladder for where it pays off — but run the creature with that quiet
confidence in mind. It is not desperate, not opportunistic, not afraid of
the party at any point. It has been doing this for longer than the
instrument has existed.

**And one of the dozen is Ferris Ambry.**

Filthy, starved, roped to a chair leg with a curtain cord, entirely alive, and
screaming at them the moment the door opens. Standing four feet from him,
wearing his face and his coat and his exact posture, is **another Ferris
Ambry**, calm and pleasant, who says hello in the party's own voice.

The real one is obvious inside a round — he's the one behaving like a
terrified accountant — but give the table its half-second.

**This is why they've been hearing his phrasing all session.** The oblex found
him in his first week and has been eating him ever since: his memories, his
voice, his rule about the doors. Everything the party has heard in Ambry's
cadence came out of this room.

**He is the objective and the complication.** Freeing him takes an action and
a cut cord. He then spends the fight under a table, and he is not brave, not
useful, and will not shut up — which is a full share of the party's attention
and entirely the point. From his side it has been **three weeks**, and it has
been four hours.

**Elder oblex** (*Volo's Guide to Monsters* — CR 10, AC 16, 115 HP printed
[10d12+50], Speed 20 ft., 5,900 XP). The duplicates are its Sulfurous
Impersonation flavour made literal — mostly set dressing; when one is
destroyed it collapses into grey slurry and the room learns something.

**One deliberate exception to Rule 3.** Printed at 115 HP, it's still not a
survivable midpoint boss for six level-5 characters — the party's typical
round-two burst clears it in about two rounds, which is over before the room
has a chance to be a scene. So the body's HP is homebrewed *further above*
printed for this fight only (see the scaling table below), and a real
fraction of the duplicates are live `Oblex Spawn` (CR ¼, *Volo's Guide to
Monsters*) rather than inert set dressing, so the room has actual action
economy instead of just a bigger health bar. Its attacks, Eat Memories,
Spellcasting, and everything else about it run exactly as printed — only the
body's hit points and the number of live spawn are house-ruled above the
printed 115. Say so if a player asks why it's tougher than they expected;
the honest answer is fine.

**How tough:** sized to survive roughly 3–4 rounds of the party's typical
burst (see **ENCOUNTER MATH**, below) rather than the ~2 rounds the printed
115 HP body would last. A genuine boss they can beat.

**It does not flee. It fights to the end and they kill it**, and you should
make the kill enormous:

> Every one of the dozen stops mid-word. They go down all at once, the way
> coats come off hooks — no bodies, just grey slurry and a smell of sulphur
> and a dozen empty sets of clothes on the floor in the shapes people were
> standing in.
>
> Except one. The last duplicate to fall — pick whichever the party hit last
> — doesn't slump like the others. It settles, almost gently, and its
> mouth keeps moving a half-second after the rest of it has stopped being a
> shape, and what comes out, in nobody's voice they recognise:
>
> *"Veyr remembers you."*
>
> Then it's slurry like the rest.
>
> And then the hum stops.
>
> For about ten seconds, for the first time since they came through the
> pantry door, the Ell is completely silent. Let the table sit in it. Then it
> starts again.

**Don't explain the line.** Nobody at the table knows who or what "Veyr" is,
including — genuinely — Hesper, when they eventually ask her. That's
correct; leave it that way until Change #12's revelation ladder pays it off
in Sessions 3–4. It should read as the single strangest thing that happens
all night, precisely because everything around it is otherwise explicable.

That silence is the level-up. It is the only time in four sessions the party
gets unambiguous proof that they can *do something* to this place.

**Loot here, on the body — real, not drafted, because the oblex itself was
never a draft:** a small stone **medallion**, worn smooth, on a cord around
where its neck would have been — unmistakably a personal holy symbol, not
survey equipment — carved with the same mark twice, identically, side by
side — see [`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md) for **the Twice-Cut Token**. Tangled
in with it, a second find: **an Echo Stone**, a smooth grey pebble that was
sitting in the ash under where the duplicates fell, warm to the touch — see
REWARDS.md. Neither means anything to the party yet. Both matter enormously
by session 4.

**What survives it:** the voices. The chalk arrows keep appearing in their
handwriting, and things keep speaking in their voices, all the way to the end
— and now there is nothing making them. Do not explain this and do not bring
the oblex back. The rematch is worth far less than the party's slowly dawning
understanding that killing it changed nothing.

*Scaling by party size only, never level. HP is homebrewed (see above); the
attacks, saves, and Eat Memories are printed as written at every size.*

| 3 | 100 | 4 | 0 | Eat Memories on alternate rounds only |
| 4 | 130 | 6 | 2 | Eat Memories on alternate rounds only |

### THE WAY HOME

Run this the moment the fight ends, before anyone suggests a rest.

The door out of the oblex chamber opens onto **the kitchen at Marchwell
House.**

The range. The rag rug. Oda's chair with the cushion crushed at one end. The
scorch on the hearthstone. It is *unmistakable*, they have been in this room,
and after two sessions of nothing being anywhere it is the first thing all
night that is where it ought to be.

Give it to them completely. Let Ambry cry. Let somebody say the words *"we're
out."* Do not hedge, do not put a chain-click in it, do not describe one wrong
detail — this beat is worth nothing if you protect them from it.

**Then somebody opens the front door.**

A hundred and forty feet of corridor with a rag rug in it.

And once they know to look: the height-notches on the pantry frame stop
partway up, and the faces in the samplers were never finished. **This is not
the house they walked in a circle back to — check the details against
session 1's degrading copies and they won't quite match.** This is a fresh
one, built new, right here, this deep, the moment the instrument had
something worth reconstructing again. That's a worse thing to learn than a
literal loop would have been: the Ell isn't running out of house to show
them. **It can make another one, flawless, at will, wherever it currently
has them.**

**And it doesn't stop at the door.** The moment that's exposed, the kitchen
starts coming apart around them while they're still standing in it —
proportions drifting, the range flattening into a wrong-scale sketch of a
range, the samplers' unfinished faces smearing into no faces at all, the
whitewash going the pale, seamless colour of something that isn't plaster.
**Staying is no longer an option; the house is failing faster than anyone
can process it.** That's what pushes them into the corridor, not a choice.

**As the last of them crosses the threshold, something screeches, once, a
very long way off** — not close, not urgent yet, but real, and unmistakably
inhuman, nothing like a scream, and clearly not a doppelganger, an oblex, or
anything they've met so far. Let it hang there unanswered. That's the
transition into Session 3: whatever's
ahead is deeper, stranger, and — for the first time — *inhabited* on purpose.

**MILESTONE: LEVEL 6.** Hand it to them right there, in the corridor, doors
still dissolving behind them. They just killed the only thing in the Ell
that could be killed and it bought them nothing at all, and they are
stronger, and those two facts sitting together — plus a scream they can't
place yet — is the exact note session 2 should end on.

## SESSION 3 — DEEPER

*Depths 3–4. **4–5 hrs.***

### Depth 3 — the wrong scale

Proportion fails, and it fails confidently. **Twelve rooms are built out
below** so you never have to improvise this depth from scratch — the party
will see roughly four to six of them in a session, depending on pacing and
which branches they pick, and the rest stay in reserve as alternates,
detours, or replacements if a table wants more.

**How it connects — a real traversal, not a wander.** The corridor out of
the collapsing false Marchwell house (end of session 2) opens onto **three
doors, side by side, each an obviously wrong height** — the geometry does
not need to obey normal Euclidean rules, but the party should always be able
to see and choose their exits rather than being told they simply arrive
somewhere. From there, follow the **Exits** line on each room below. Every
room has at least one exit that goes deeper; **all paths eventually converge
on Room 5, the drafted orchard**, which is the fixed node the party cannot
avoid and which leads on to Depth 4.

eventually.

**1. The Nursery.** One-third scale, perfect in every detail down to a
half-finished sampler on a tiny frame. The party doesn't shrink to fit — they
simply don't fit, and have to crouch, crawl, or break furniture to move
through it at all, which is its own kind of horror after three sessions of
rooms that were merely wrong, not physically obstructive.

**2. The Ceiling Parlour.** Furnished, fire lit, gravity pointing at the
ceiling instead of the floor. Stepping through the doorway means falling
*up* twelve feet onto a rug. Getting back down is easy — the doorway they
came through still has ordinary gravity on the other side of its threshold —
but the horror is watching a teammate stand on a ceiling, upside down
relative to everyone still in the corridor, having an entirely normal
conversation about it.

**3. The Five-Hundred-Foot Hall.** A single straight hallway, five hundred
feet of it, one window at the far end showing the orchard in the wrong
season. **Optional fight: the Late Cats** (see *Two avoidable fights*,
below) doze across the path at the halfway mark.

**4. The Stair Room.** No floors, no landings — stairs meeting stairs at
every angle, going up in every direction including down. Climbing any of
them long enough, in any direction, eventually deposits the party on a
landing that opens directly onto Room 5. There is no wrong staircase here,
only a disorienting one.

**5. The Drafted Orchard** *(fixed node — every branch leads here)*. An
orchard, indoors, in a hall with no visible end: wood-coloured measured
space, trees at exactly correct spacing, no bark on any of them, and a
still, birdless quiet that anyone who was in session 1 will recognise
instantly — *this is what was outside the windows.* That recognition is the
single best continuity beat in the campaign; give them a moment with it —
**and then let somebody spot the still bear**, motionless between two trees
a hundred feet off. (See *Two avoidable fights*, below. The orchard is a
place; the owlbears in it are optional and skippable.) The far end of the
orchard, however the party reaches it, is the way down to Depth 4.

**6. The Long Larder.** A larder hallway lined with shelved preserves that
get visibly, steadily smaller toward the far end — jars the size of a fist,
then a thumbnail, then a grain of salt, arranged with total consistency.
**Walking its length does not shrink anyone or anything they carry** — the
far door, reached in the ordinary number of steps, is a completely normal
size once you're standing in front of it. It's a rendering error made
physical, not a transformation, and a curious player who checks (measuring
their own boots against the "shrunk" floor, say) can work that out for
themselves. **Optional find:** a genuinely tiny, genuinely real preserve jar
near the midpoint, still sized however small it looked from a distance even
after being carried back out to normal-scaled rooms — a harmless, sellable
curiosity (a few gp to the right collector, nothing more).

**7. The Waiting Room of Twins.** Two identical armchairs face each other
across a low tea table, laid for two. A mirror on the far wall reflects the
room — and whoever sits in either chair sees their reflection sit down
roughly **a half-second late**, then perform a slightly different small
gesture than the one they actually made. Nothing hostile; a demonstration
that time itself has started running thick and inexact down here, the same
way space already has. Sitting and simply watching the delay for a minute is
free, safe, and deeply unsettling — spend it if the table wants atmosphere.

**8. The Match-Struck Hall.** A long hall in perpetual near-dark. Striking a
light — a match, a cantrip, a lantern's flame catching — reveals, for that
one instant only, that the hall's true proportions are wrong: walls a third
too close, a ceiling a third too low, doorways subtly displaced from where
they appeared in the dark. The moment the light goes out or steadies, the
hall looks ordinary again. Nothing attacks here; it's pure atmosphere, and a
party that keeps a light burning continuously the whole way through gets to
watch the wrongness resolve into steady, held focus instead of flickering
in and out — a small, earned mercy if anyone thinks to try it.

**9. The Weighing Hall.** Dominated by an oversized merchant's balance-scale,
crated at the centre of the room. One pan holds a smooth stone stamped with
a number; the other is empty. **Setting any object in the empty pan tips the
scale to a new, different number, chalked automatically on a slate
underneath** — the room measuring whatever's offered it, indifferent to
what the object actually is. Nothing bad happens if the party experiments;
it's the clearest hands-on demonstration in Depth 3 of the instrument
treating everything, always, as a quantity to be recorded and nothing else.

**10. The Shrinking Doors.** A short corridor of doors that get visibly
smaller toward the far end, the last one doll-sized. **Every door, at
whatever size it appears, opens onto a completely normal-sized room or
corridor beyond it** — matching Room 6's lesson from the opposite angle
(the *doors* lie about scale here, rather than the shelves). A party that's
already solved the Long Larder will recognise the trick immediately and
should be allowed to say so.

**11. The Held-Breath Room.** A plain room where sound arrives several
seconds late. Speak, and hear yourself a few seconds afterward, faintly
wrong — a half-tone off, or in a voice not quite matching. Time is
audibly thick here in a way session 3 has otherwise only shown visually
(the Waiting Room of Twins). No danger; let the party talk to themselves
across the delay if they want to.

**12. The Cutter's Rest — the depth's major discovery.** A small side
chamber built to ordinary, human scale — the only room in Depth 3 that
isn't wrong, which itself should feel wrong after everything else. A
long-cold campfire ring of stones. A rolled, rotted bedroll. A tin cup.
**Coiled in the cold ash: a small stone medallion on a worn cord**, carved
with a single mark cut twice, identically, side by side — clearly a
personal holy symbol, worn smooth by years against skin, not survey
equipment — see [`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md) for **the Twice-Cut Token**, if
the party hasn't already found one on the oblex. Tucked under the bedroll,
though, is the genuine article: **a brass-jointed folding rule**, the kind
a working surveyor keeps on their own person rather than with the rest of
the kit — pitted, tarnished, decades of honest use on it, its joints still
swinging true and its graduations still sharp. Stamped small along its
inner edge, worn but legible: **THE MEASURE**, over a maker's mark cut
twice, identically, side by side. This is the first time that name appears
anywhere in the adventure, on a tool that is unmistakably human-made and
unmistakably old. Don't explain it. This is deliberately somebody's
campsite from a very long time ago — see Change #15, below, for whose.

*Scaling: 3–4 PCs — run six of the twelve rooms (the entry three plus three
picks toward the orchard); the rest are cut, not simplified. 5–6 PCs — run
eight to ten.*

### Two avoidable fights

Both are **genuinely avoidable and visibly so** — telegraphed a room early,
with an obvious way past. Both are easy for six characters at level 6. The
point is to keep the Ell feeling dangerous without spending an hour on it, and
to let the party feel clever for going around. If they'd rather fight, let
them; it costs resources and nothing else.

**The late cats.** Two **displacer beasts** (CR 3 each) in the five-hundred-foot
hallway, dozing across the only path. Their displacement is *temporal* —
they're seeing them a second and a half ago, so neither is ever quite where
the arrow goes.

*Telegraph:* they see both from four hundred feet away, and both are asleep,
and they have a very long time to decide. *Avoid:* territorial about the
hallway and nothing else. Back out, take a fold, come round. Neither pursues
past the doorway. *Fight:* easy for six characters, but every miss is
unnerving and two of them means twice the attacks if it goes long, and the
noise brings the next thing.

**The still bears.** Three **owlbears** (CR 3 each) in the drafted orchard —
an orchard indoors, wood-coloured, correct spacing, no bark on any tree. They
are perfect in every particular and none of them breathes or blinks.

*Telegraph:* they stand motionless between the trees, spread out, and can be
watched for as long as anyone likes. None is asleep. They are simply not
doing anything, because nothing has measured them doing anything yet.
*Avoid:* they react to movement toward them, not past them. Walk the outer
rows and none stirs. *Fight:* straightforward for six characters at level 6,
and afterwards there's nothing inside any of them.

*Scaling: at 3–4 PCs, run one of the two, not both.*

### More presences (planted set-pieces, not fights)

None of these is meant to *end* in a fight, even when the party starts one.
They're texture between the two avoidable fights and the rule-rooms — run
the followers as a slow-burn thread across as much of depth 3 as the session
allows, and drop the quoting thing in whichever room fits.

**The followers.** Not one intellect devourer that followed something in and
got stuck — **three, in sequence, and they are not lost.** They are Veyr's,
here to watch, imitate, and catalogue the party the way the oblex catalogued
Ambry, and the escalation across the three is the whole point: **killing the
observer does not stop the observation.**

- **First follower.** A small scuffing sound that stops when anyone turns
  round. It keeps twenty, thirty feet back, out of easy light, and has
  learned to copy one character's walk — the same hitch in the same
  character's stride, from behind, in the dark. Let them glimpse it two or
  three times before anyone gets a clean look. **If cornered or attacked, it
  does nothing at all** — no attack, no Reaction, no attempt to flee. It
  simply stands there and is killed. Run its printed intellect devourer stats
  for the purpose of HP and any damage it might incidentally take, but it
  never once acts against the party. This should feel wrong in a way combat
  never has before: nothing this dangerous-looking has ever just *let* them.
- **Second follower.** The moment the party moves on, they hear another one
  behind them — same scuffing gait-copy, same distance, plainly not the one
  they just killed (if they check the body, it's still there). It behaves
  identically to the first, including the same non-resistance if attacked.
  Two in a row is the point where a table should start finding this
  unsettling rather than convenient.
- **Third follower — only if they killed the second too.** It behaves
  differently from the start: it never lets its distance drop below thirty
  feet, effortlessly matching any pace the party sets, and it does not
  approach even when the party stops moving entirely. **If it is ever
  reduced to 0 hit points, it does not die.** It simply isn't there anymore
  — no body, no slurry, nothing to loot — and turns up again, distant and
  watching, within the hour. There is no version of this fight the party can
  finish. Let them work that out themselves; don't say it outright.

**Play all three utterly without malice.** They never attack first, never
raise their voice, never react to being spoken to beyond the copied gait.
The horror is purely that something is here on purpose, has been the whole
time, and cannot be made to stop by force. (What "Veyr" means is not
something the party can learn from the followers themselves — see Change
#12's revelation ladder for where that comes together.)

**The quoting thing.** A **gibbering mouther** (CR 2), encountered filling a
side room or a dead-end corridor, motionless until approached. It says only
the last true thing each character said aloud — verbatim, in their own
voice — on a loop, softly, whichever line it heard most recently. It is the
same recording-and-playback mechanism the party has been living inside since
session 2, given a body. It does not seek the party out and does not chase;
if left alone it goes on quoting them to an empty room. If provoked into a
fight, run it as printed.

### Depth 4 — the rules

Physics becomes a property of the room. One substituted law per room, obeyed
absolutely, because the instrument recorded a behaviour and never recorded the
reason for it. **State the rule plainly when they work it out. Don't be coy.**
Let them work it out through trial first — that's the point of the room — but
if a table is stuck and getting frustrated rather than curious, an
Investigation check (**DC 13**) lets a character articulate the rule outright
instead of continuing to guess.

Each room below gives you the exact rule, what it looks like walking in, what
the first likely interaction produces, how repeated testing reveals the rule,
what happens at the edges, the intended way through, and at least one
alternate solution a clever party can find on their own. **These are rules,
not puzzles the party solves once and discards** — let them use any of the
six for their own ends for the rest of the adventure, not just to get through
the room they're standing in.

#### The Hanging

**Rule:** anything released inside this room stays exactly where it was let
go — the height, the position, forever. Nothing here falls.

**On entry:** a long, hazy hall. Dozens of ordinary objects hang motionless
in mid-air at head height and above, strung out into the distance like beads
on an invisible line — a lantern, a boot, a hat, a coil of rope, a child's
ball, a half-empty cup tipped mid-pour with its contents frozen in a
glass-still arc. Nothing supports any of it.

**First interaction:** somebody drops something, on purpose or by accident.
It does not fall. It simply stays where their hand let go of it.

**How the rule shows itself:** two objects released from different heights
hang at their own two heights, not the same one — proving it's about the
point of release, not a floor at some fixed level. A thrown object continues
its arc until the exact instant it would have left the thrower's control,
then stops there, hanging along the arc's remaining curve like a paused
throw.

**Edge cases:** releasing something while walking leaves it hanging at that
exact point in space, not following the mover. Pouring a liquid freezes it
mid-stream, rigid and glass-like. Letting go of a *living* creature (a
willing ally floated or lowered) does **not** hang them — the rule reads
"released," and nothing alive in this campaign should count as an object; say
so if a player tries.

**The way through:** there is no forced barrier here — it's a loot room and a
toy, not a gate. If you want one: a low gap or drop elsewhere in the room can
be crossed by *deliberately* dropping objects at calculated heights to build
a suspended, stair-like line across it, then climbing the hanging objects
like rungs.

**Alternate solution, and the one worth remembering:** anything can be safely
stored here, forever, in the open, immune to theft, decay, or the fold rule —
which is exactly the trap discussed in "The strongboxes," below: 800 gold
pieces or two free hands, never both. A party that returns here later to
retrieve a cache has understood the room completely.

**Loot here:** the Held Thing — see [`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md).

#### The Watched Door

**Rule:** a doorway exists, and can be crossed, only during a moment when at
least **two** people are looking directly at it. Location doesn't matter —
both watchers can be on the same side, split one to each side, or one watcher
plus the person crossing looking at it themselves. Count and simultaneity are
all that matter.

**On entry:** an ordinary-looking doorframe in a corridor wall. Looked at by
only one person, it wavers — a heat-shimmer outline, present but somehow
unconvincing, like a shape glimpsed and then doubted.

**First interaction:** a lone character stares at it and gets the shimmer,
nothing more. **Two people look at once** — anywhere, any combination — and
it solidifies into a real, solid, crossable door for as long as both keep
looking.

**How the rule shows itself:** have them test it deliberately — one watcher,
shimmer; two watchers, door; either watcher looks away, wall. Let them
confirm this three or four times before it's load-bearing.

**Edge cases:** if the door reverts to wall while someone is mid-crossing —
because a watcher was startled, blinded, or simply looked away — **that
character does not get crushed or trapped.** Rule it as a hard shove back to
whichever side they were more than halfway from; shaken, not harmed, and
free to try again. A drafted resident or an animal told to "watch" the door
does **not** count — the rule wants genuine attention, not eyes pointed at
it.

**The way through:** one person stays on the near side watching while the
rest cross one at a time, each crosser plus the watcher satisfying the count.
**The last two people can cross together**, both looking at the door as they
step through — they need nobody else.

**Alternate solution:** a reflection counts as looking, if the party thinks
to use a mirror or still water — letting one physically present person
effectively watch from an angle they couldn't stand in, useful if the
corridor geometry makes a normal two-watcher split awkward.

#### The Slag Room

**Rule:** sound above a murmur becomes solid matter. The louder and longer
the noise, the more of it, and the more dangerous.

**On entry:** a bare stone chamber. Heaped in every corner: dark, rounded,
slag-like lumps, centuries old, worn glassy-smooth — old shouted arguments,
solidified and still faintly legible as shapes if you look closely (a fist,
a snarl, once in a great while an actual half-formed word).

**First interaction:** somebody talks at a normal, conversational volume —
nothing happens, which is the important first data point. Somebody raises
their voice or calls out — **a half-second later, dark, rough, gravel-sized
nodules erupt from the air near where the sound was loudest** and clatter to
the floor. A full shout produces fist-sized, sharp-edged chunks that fly a
short distance in roughly the direction the voice carried, hard enough to
sting — treat a shout or a spell with a loud verbal component as dealing
**1d4 bludgeoning** to anyone within 10 feet of the speaker, no save, once
per shout.

**How the rule shows itself:** volume scales the effect predictably —
whispering, nothing; talking, nothing; raised voice, harmless pebbles;
shouting or screaming, real shrapnel. Let the party discover the gradient by
testing it themselves rather than stating the DCs of "loud" up front.

**Edge cases and the fun part:** a word spoken clearly, deliberately, and
**exactly the same way more than once** sometimes crystallizes into slag
that vaguely resembles what it named — shout "KEY" three times in a row and
the resulting lump is crude, roughly key-shaped, and will turn a simple lock
**once**, badly, before crumbling. This is a genuine tool, not a joke option
— but it is never fine, never reliable, and never more than a single crude
use, the same as any ambitious use of the Slate below.

**The way through:** cross at a whisper or in absolute silence; nothing
stops a quiet party. This room is a behavioural constraint, not a locked
door — the danger is entirely self-inflicted.

**Alternate solution:** deliberately generating word-slag as improvised
ammunition, a crude tool, or (with the "shout the same word three times"
trick above) a one-use object the party actually needs and doesn't have.
Reward genuine cleverness here; punish nothing except carelessness.

#### The Long Way

**Rule:** motion directly toward the room's far door produces no progress at
all. Any indirect motion — along a wall, in a spiral, backing away while
still angling toward it, circling the room's perimeter — produces real
progress, measured in relative distance to fixed landmarks along the way.

**On entry:** a long room or hall with the exit door plainly visible at the
far end, looking like an easy, unremarkable walk.

**First interaction:** the party (or one scout) walks straight at the door.
After what should be more than enough steps to reach it, **it is exactly as
far away as it started** — not receding, not approaching, simply unmoved
relative to them.

**How the rule shows itself:** someone frustrated walks along a side wall
instead (to inspect something, to pace it off, out of sheer irritation) and
**the door is visibly, measurably closer relative to a landmark on that
wall** by the time they reach the far corner. A full circuit of the room's
perimeter brings the door about a quarter of the remaining distance closer
each time — concrete, repeatable, and something a player can test on
purpose once they suspect it.

**Edge cases:** any non-direct motion counts, not just wall-hugging —
walking backward while angling toward the door, a wide spiral toward the
centre, even side-stepping in a zigzag all bank progress at roughly the same
rate. Only a dead-straight approach vector produces nothing.

**The way through:** three or four full or partial circuits of the room's
perimeter, in either direction, reaches the door. It takes longer than the
room's straight-line size suggests it should, and that's the entire point —
not a trick to be clever about once and forget, but a rule that keeps
working if they remember it in a later room shaped like this one.

**Alternate solution:** a party that works out the underlying logic
("indirect approach banks progress; direct approach doesn't") rather than
memorising "hug the wall" specifically will correctly generalise it to any
room built the same way later in the Ell, which is the actual reward for
understanding it instead of just passing it.

#### The Unanimous Room

**Rule:** everyone conscious and present must be attempting the identical
action, at the same moment, or **nobody can act or move at all.**

**On entry:** an ordinary room, except that the instant everyone is inside,
legs stop answering. Not paralysis exactly — more like every individual
intention arrives a half-second too separate from everyone else's to
resolve into motion.

**First interaction:** someone tries to step forward. Nothing happens.
Someone else tries at the same time, in a different direction. Still
nothing — different actions don't count as unanimous even if simultaneous.

**How the rule shows itself:** it is common, and worth letting happen
almost by accident, for the whole party to try the *same* thing at once
early on — everyone draws a weapon at the sound of something, say — and for
one half-second, everyone can move. Let them notice that themselves before
explaining anything.

**Edge cases:** an unconscious, restrained, or otherwise non-acting
creature doesn't count toward the requirement — only conscious, mobile
people present need to match. Similar-but-not-identical actions (raising a
weapon vs. drawing one) don't satisfy it; it wants the same verb, not the
same spirit. Ambry, if he's with them, is a genuine complication here —
panic makes him bad at doing what everyone else is doing, on purpose,
consistently, all the way through the room.

**The way through:** someone calls a cadence out loud — *"On three: step
forward with your left foot"* — and the party moves as one on a count. Not
hard once understood; the puzzle is entirely in recognising the rule exists.

**Alternate solution:** the rule freezes *anyone* present, including a
hostile creature that follows them in or is lured inside — a pursuer that
doesn't do exactly what the party is doing is just as stuck as they'd be.
A party that lures something dangerous in here and then holds still,
unanimously, has turned a trap into a cage. Cut into the plaster above the
far door, in a neat surveyor's hand: **ALL AS ONE OR NONE FOR ALL.**

#### The Slate

**Rule:** whatever is written on the room's slate is true, for as long as
it's written there, regardless of what was true a moment before.

**On entry:** a plain slate on the wall, a stub of chalk on a string beside
it. It currently reads: *THE DOOR IS LOCKED* — and the door is.

**First interaction:** erasing the statement entirely unlocks the door, at
once, cleanly. This is the intended solution and it should feel almost too
easy the first time.

**How the rule shows itself:** a small, safe demonstration — writing
something modest and true-adjacent about the room itself (*THE CANDLE IS
LIT*, if there's an unlit candle nearby) makes it so, instantly and without
complication, so the party can trust the rule before betting anything large
on it.

**Edge cases and the ambitious-statement rule:** only one statement can be
true at a time — writing something new while the old line still stands
simply doesn't take until the old one is erased. A modest, physically local
claim (about this room, this door, an object actually present) works
cleanly and reliably. **An ambitious claim** — something grand, something
about a place or person not physically here, something the party is
plainly trying to abuse — still happens, **once, and badly**: it works
crookedly, incompletely, or with a strange cost (a duplicate left behind, a
shadow that doesn't match anymore, the slate cracking so it can never be
written on again this crossing). Decide the specific complication in the
moment to fit what they wrote; never simply refuse it outright.

**The way through:** erase *THE DOOR IS LOCKED.*

**Alternate solution:** carrying the memory of this room forward. A party
that hauls a spare chalk stub back to the Slate later, or that remembers a
locked door *elsewhere* might respond to the same trick if they can get
something written near it, has understood the Ell rather than merely
solved a room.

**Coordinate these rooms with the revelation ladder where it's useful** —
none of the six above requires outside lore to solve, and none should ever
be gated behind it. Any Veyr- or Measure-related evidence placed in or near
a rule room (see the Cutter's Rest, Room 12 of the Depth 3 bank, for the
model) should sit *alongside* the puzzle, never as a required key to it.

**Loot here:** the Held Thing, in the Hanging — see [`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md).

### The drafted people

Depth 4 is inhabited — not by copies of the party, but by the instrument's
attempts at **residents**, made to test whether the rooms work.

- A woman at a basin washing a plate that is already clean, and will be
  washing it tomorrow.
- Nine people at a dinner table mid-conversation, all saying the correct
  *kind* of thing and none of it meaning anything.
- A man in the Hanging holding a cup, who has been holding it a very long
  time, and who is **so glad to see them.**

They are not hostile. They are polite. They answer questions incorrectly. They
are furniture. This is where the party stops fearing what's chasing them and
starts fearing the place.

### The real Cass *(optional, and worth doing)*

In a drafted kitchen at the edge of depth 4, sitting in a chair that is nearly
his chair: **Cass Marchwell**, a month gone, thin, entirely himself and
entirely calm about it. He walked in one night to see where the corridor went
and could not find his way back. He has worked out roughly what took his place
and he is not angry about it — mostly he wants to know whether his sister has
been managing the pressing.

He is a real man, he can walk out with them, and he costs nothing to bring
along. **He is also the payoff for the very first scene, and the payoff is
better than "it wasn't measured upstairs."**

**The real answer (Change #14):** ask him about the stairs, or let him bring
it up himself, dry and a little embarrassed: his hip has been bad for well
over a year. He gave up sleeping upstairs long before any of this started —
he's kept to the kitchen chair, night after night, for longer than Oda likes
to admit. *"It doesn't go upstairs, does it. I never was much upstairs
myself, not this last while."*

**That's the whole answer, and it's worse than a missing file.** The draft
in the kitchen wasn't an incomplete copy of Cass because the instrument
failed to measure the first floor. It was a *complete* copy of the man the
Ell had actually, repeatedly, exhaustively measured — and that man had
already reduced himself to a downstairs life before anyone ever drafted him.
The Ell didn't get Cass wrong. **It got him exactly right, and what it got
right was smaller than the whole person.** Say this plainly if the party
reaches for it; it's the first time the adventure's central mechanism (drafts
reproduce *behavior*, not identity) becomes personal rather than abstract,
and it should land quietly rather than as a triumphant explanation.

**If they bring him home**, Oda gets her brother back — and the party has to
decide, on the ride out, whether to tell him what they killed four of in a
kitchen on the first night. He will ask. He noticed they went quiet.

### Hesper Wick

She arrives when the party is stuck in a rule-room, and she solves it in about
nine seconds.

**Hesper Wick, the Chainwoman.** A tithe-surveyor who walked into a door like
this one, in a different house, two valleys over, a hundred and some years
ago. Practical, dry, courteous, unsentimental. Wears her chain coiled at her
hip and has never once put it down. She is the first thing in three sessions
that knows what the rules are before the party walks into them.

**She helps. Genuinely. All of it is true.** She knows the rule-rooms, the
fold rule, the safe places to rest, and the way down. She likes them. Play her
as an ally without reservation and without foreshadowing, because the turn
lands hardest if you never sold it.

*Voice: answers the question actually asked, never the one implied. Measures
things with her eyes and says the number without being asked.*

**If they show her Tobin's notebook, or mention "TRUST NO ONE" out loud:**
she goes quiet for a moment, then admits it's hers. She found a frightened,
counting boy four days into a wander of his own, a while back by her count,
pointed him at a fold she knew led home, and wrote the one thing she had time
for in the only ink she had — scraped ash and substrate-dust from an old
rule-room, mixed with water. *"I couldn't explain the rest before he had to
go. I still think about whether one line was enough."* This is optional and
should feel like a small, real gift if the party thinks to ask — not a
required beat, and not something she volunteers unprompted.

**If they show her the Twice-Cut Token:** she's seen the mark before —
scratched small into the corner of an old rule-room, worn into the handle
of a chain-rig she once found and stripped for parts, always exactly like
this, always in twos. *"I took it for a maker's stamp. Every smith's proud
of the same tool twice, I thought."* She's wrong, and she knows it now if
the party's worked out what the doubled mark actually means — but she was
alone with that guess for a century, and nobody ever corrected her. Let this
land as one more piece of her isolation, not a lecture.

**Reintroducing the Late Cats (Change #15), if the party avoided them
earlier:** if the two displacer beasts in the five-hundred-foot hall were
fought and killed rather than avoided, skip this entirely. If they were
avoided, they can return once, late in the session, after Hesper has joined
— and this time they go straight for **her**, not the party. She recognises
them on sight, a half-second before anyone asks why she's suddenly tense:
*"These two took three fingers off my left hand, the winter I learned not
to sleep near open ground. Grew back wrong. I've disliked them ever since."*
Let her show a genuine old scar or an odd habit (she counts her own fingers
under her breath sometimes, which nobody's ever asked her about) that this
retroactively explains. Run the fight as a normal two-beast encounter with
Hesper as a competent ally combatant rather than a bystander — it isn't
harder than the original optional version, just personal, and it's the last
easy fight before the finale. Don't force this if the session is already
running long; it's a texture beat, not a requirement.

**Tier 2 profile:** Veteran-like if it comes to it; expertise in Investigation
and Survival; passive Perception 16. Full combat profile in session 4.

**End the session** with Hesper leading them somewhere the walls have stopped
pretending.

## SESSION 4 — OUT

*Depth 5. **4–5 hrs.***

### The substrate

**How to describe it, concretely.**

Furniture stops. Wallpaper stops. Then the *materials* stop — floor, wall and
ceiling become one continuous pale surface with no boards, no seams and no
joins, the colour of dry clay, faintly warm, and it goes on in every
direction.

**Cut into it, everywhere, is a technical drawing.** Incised lines, arcs, and
groups of tally marks, from fingernail-fine to a foot deep, overlapping at a
dozen different scales like a surveyor's field book scratched onto the inside
of a cathedral. It is not decorative and it is not writing. It is a **plan** —
the kind a chain-crew produces — carved into the world at full size.

**And anyone who knows the vale can read it — build the recognition in three
explicit stages, each one wider than the last, so the party arrives at the
conclusion themselves rather than being told it.**

**Stage one: "this is Marchwell."** Nothing here is drawn as itself — a tree
isn't a tree, it's a punched dot with three numbers beside it (height, girth,
distance to the next dot); a hedge is one scored line with a figure every so
often where it changes; a wall or drainage ditch is a long incised groove
with thickness ticks. Let the party match this notation against things
they've physically stood in front of:

> The floor of the hall is a hundred feet across and there's a map cut into
> it. There's the farmhouse footprint, to scale, down to the press-house and
> the ash-house. There's the lane they rode up. There's the orchard, forty
> dots in rows — and one of them has a longer tally than the others, because
> it's the big pear tree in Oda Marchwell's yard, and somebody measured it,
> once, carefully, a long time before any of you were born.

**Stage two: "this is the land around Marchwell."** Widen the frame without
changing the notation style — the same dots, lines, and figures simply keep
going past the farmhouse's edge, because the survey never stopped at a
property line:

> The pattern doesn't stop at the orchard wall. It keeps going: the sunken
> lane toward Tilbrook, a scored line with a grade noted every furlong. The
> mill bend on the Ammet, a single unbroken curve with depths notched along
> it. The ford below Tilbrook. A cluster of footprint-outlines and height
> marks that can only be Tilbrook itself.

**Stage three: "this is Ashgarden Vale."** Widen again, using names the
characters could plausibly know or have heard on the road — **the South
Road**, **Orchardmere** and its lake, **Saint Veddow's Rest**, the hamlets of
**Nettlecombe** and **Marrow Cross** — each rendered the same way: roads as
paired incised lines with distances, settlements as dense clusters of
footprint-outlines and height marks, hills as nested contour cuts, woods as
repeating trunk-dots with canopy arcs. **This is the moment to say the
conclusion out loud if nobody has:** the Ell has measured far more of the
Material Plane than one farmhouse — an entire named region, obsessively, at
a scale that took a very long time and a great many hands.

**Optional deeper stage, gated behind a check rather than the core
revelation:** an Investigation or Arcana check (**DC 18**) traces the
notation further still, past the edge of anything anyone at the table
recognises — proof the survey didn't stop at the vale's borders either, and
a hook for a future one-shot or campaign thread, never resolved here.

**And here they watch it working.** Ahead of them, in the bare floor, a line
opens — a fresh incision, cut clean, travelling at a walking pace. There is
nothing holding a tool. It is scoring their own route into the plan as they
make it, and where they stop, it stops, and puts a dot and three figures
beside it. One of the figures is somebody's height.

*This is the only "sighting" of the instrument in the adventure, and it is the
correct one: not a machine, not a face — **a line being drawn.** If a player
puts a hand over the floor ahead of it, it goes round the hand and notes the
hand.*

Then, cut *under* that and slightly out of register, the same country **with
no orchards in it.** Under that, no hedges. Under that, no roads, no field
walls, no ford — a river and some hills and a great deal of nothing, measured
just as carefully as the rest.

And the **count of previous surveys**, in tally, which takes a genuinely long
time to finish reading and is not a small number.

**The Mountains of Madness beat is here and it is not a monster.** It's the
moment the party works out the history of the place and the history is worse
than anything that chased them. Let them do the reading. Don't summarise it
for them. An Investigation or Arcana check (**DC 13** for the general shape of
it, **DC 18** to estimate how many other surveys the tally represents) just
tells them how much more there is — it never gates the reading itself, only
how precisely they can quantify it.

Scratched into the substrate in every language it has ever copied, including
two nobody can read: **HOW BIG IS A HOME?**

**Loot here:** the Cut Page, prised loose from the substrate — see
[`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md).

### The reconstruction station — the answer, guaranteed

**This is a mandatory revelation. Build it around evidence every table gets,
regardless of what any individual party actually did or said — player-made
chalk marks and remembered jokes are welcome bonus texture, never the
load-bearing proof.**

A side-chamber off the substrate hall, its walls the same pale, warm
material — except here it isn't still. A piece of the Measure's actual
equipment stands in the middle of it: an articulated frame of chains,
lenses, and gauges, sweeping back and forth across a fixed arc, in no hurry,
taking the same reading over and over. **It isn't building anything. It's
measuring — and it isn't succeeding.** A few feet in front of it, the wall
itself is remaking itself, over and over, on its own. The frame doesn't
cause that. It never could. It's simply failed, every single pass, to get
one clean, final reading of something that will not hold still.

**The substrate is ceaselessly assembling a version of the Marchwell
kitchen, over and over, whether or not the equipment is even pointed at
it.** No visible hands, no construction — just the Echo's own constant,
native change, taking this recorded shape because this is what's been
measured here, exhaustively, for the better part of a year: incisions draw
themselves into a floor-sized outline of the kitchen; a crude, featureless
grey shape rises where the range should be, then refines, gains
proportion, gains the shadow of a cooking surface; a second grey shape
resolves into something with roughly Cass's height and the memory of a
cardigan's texture. **The party has already seen this exact kitchen, from
the inside, in session 1** — that's the point. Use only events guaranteed to
have happened in every playthrough: the range, the rag rug, Cass shutting
the door, the corridor the front door opened onto, Ambry's presence
somewhere in the wider record. If the party made a distinctive chalk mark or
said something memorable, let it surface here too, precisely, alongside the
guaranteed material — a genuine bonus, never required for the scene to
land.

**Watch it fail, and try again anyway, on its own.** The Cass-shape gets
the cardigan wrong twice before it's right; the first two attempts simply
stop being — collapsing back into the wall's pale material without
ceremony, no drama, nothing correcting anything on purpose. It's not being
corrected. It's just changing, again, the way it never stops changing.
**This is the single clearest demonstration in the adventure of how the
whole place actually works: the equipment doesn't copy finished rooms — it
watches a wall that keeps remaking itself, badly, then less badly, forever,
completely on its own, and fails to pin any single version of it down.
Nothing here was ever built, by the equipment or by anyone else currently
present. It simply won't stop happening.** A chalk arrow is data the same
way a doorframe's height is data — something the equipment recorded, once,
among countless other things — but recording it never made this wall move.
The wall was always going to keep moving. The party has spent three
sessions marking a place that was already remaking itself before they
arrived and will go on doing so after they leave.

**The physical collision, and the revelation ladder's stage 9:** built into
—not beside, *into*—the reconstruction station's far wall is something the
station clearly did not make and is not making: a fist-sized fragment of
worked stone, half-swallowed by the pale substrate material as if it grew up
around it rather than the reverse, carved with the same mark cut twice,
identically, side by side that the party may already have from the oblex or
the Cutter's Rest. **This is mortal work and something older, physically
occupying the same few feet of space.** Don't explain which came first;
let the image do it — a measuring-instrument built around, into, and
oblivious to something that was already here.

**If the party has the Twice-Cut Token, this is where it pays off.** Let
them think to hold it up against the wall-carving themselves before you
confirm anything — same mark, same proportions, same everything. Once they
have, this is the closest the adventure comes to actually explaining what
"Veyr" is, without ever saying the name: **the mark was never a face, a
letter, or a maker's stamp. It's the rule, drawn as a picture of itself —
two things, cut identically, side by side, that are actually one thing.**
Whatever wears this symbol, whatever it represents, its whole nature is the
same law the party has been living inside since the front door shut.
That's not a coincidence to solve. It's the answer, delivered as an image
instead of a sentence — say only as much as that, and let the table draw
the rest.

**The voices work the same way, on sound instead of shape.** The equipment
has measured and recorded every word anyone has ever said here, and the
wall's own ceaseless remaking folds those recordings back in on its own,
because a room where someone spoke is a room whose constant, independent
change now includes speech as one more thing it never stops reshaping.
**And that's what the oblex was
doing at the midpoint** — a real, native thing that found this file a long
time ago and learned to live off what it contained, which is why its
duplicates spoke in voices and phrasings it had no other way to know. It was
reading the file, not writing it. Killing it didn't stop the file, and this
room is the file, still running.

Hesper can say the whole thing in two sentences if the party doesn't
assemble it themselves: *"You've been marking a place that copies marks.
You've been chasing yourselves since Tuesday."* She still has no name for
whatever's carved into that wall fragment, and says so plainly if asked —
that part is for the party, or a scholar back home, to work out later.

### The door, and Hesper

The room is a wide stretch of substrate, the plan cut into the floor, three
low ridges of varying height and width breaking it into uneven thirds. Off
to one side stand two squared-off pillars, waist-to-shoulder height,
identical to each other in size and carving — a matched pair, like
everything else down here that comes in twos. Nobody will look twice at
either. Don't call attention to them. Never mention them again until one
moves. (See **THE OTHER PILLAR**, below — one of them is a Roper.)

The way out is real and she takes them to it. And then:

**Count the party at the table before you run this**, and use the real number.
Baseline: six PCs + Ambry + Cass (if rescued) = **eight open scratches, nine
people standing at the door.** If they left Cass, it's seven and eight. The
arithmetic is the whole scene, so get it right out loud.

> "Eight open marks on that frame. Him, the old man, and six of you. That's
> the count it took and that's the count it gives back." She's already got her
> chain in her hand. "I came in through a different house, by a door that
> isn't there any more. I have never once been on anyone's list. I've waited a
> hundred and four years for a party big enough that one of you might be
> left."

She is sorry. She means it. She fights anyway.

**And she is not alone.** Standing behind her, one for every long rest the
party took: **copies of the party.** Same faces, same gear, same way of
standing — and they are not silent. **They talk.** Not to each other, not
tactically: they echo, flatly, in the party's own voices, things the party
has actually said since session 1 — the joke about the hats, what somebody
swore at the oblex, what somebody promised Ambry. One line per copy, on its
turn or when it drops, never more — this is seasoning, not narration, and it
should never slow the fight down. She didn't make them and doesn't command
them — the instrument did, out of everything it's recorded them saying since
the pantry door, and they are simply *here*, because this is where the party
is.

**Doppelgangers** (CR 3), one per long rest, **cap 5**; past the cap, give
Hesper +15 HP each instead. They fight competently, without flourish, and
they do not use the fold — they don't need to, they're already where they
need to be.

*A party that rested after every fight walks into five of themselves. A party
that pushed through arrives on fumes to a fair fight. Both are earned, and
both tables will understand instantly why.*

**Hesper Wick — combat profile.** Base: **veteran** (*Monster Manual*, CR 3)
with these changes, in a room she has had a century to learn:
- **Medium humanoid. AC 17, HP 110, Speed 30 ft.** Proficiency +4 (up from
  printed +2 — a century of practice). STR 18 (+4), DEX 13 (+1), CON 14 (+2),
  INT 10 (+0), WIS 11 (+0), CHA 10 (+0). Passive Perception 12. Languages
  Common. **Multiattack:** two Longsword attacks, **+8 to hit, 1d8+4
  slashing** each.
- **Fold (once per turn, no action, part of her movement):** she enters a
  doorway and exits an identical one anywhere in the room. She has memorised
  every duplicate feature in it, so to her the room is not a rectangle — it's
  a set of doorways that are secretly the same doorway, and she knows which.
  It's mobility, not a puzzle to solve: use it to keep her from getting
  cornered or focus-fired, and to reposition after a bad exchange. Nothing
  more elaborate than that.
- **Morale:** she does not flee and she does not stop talking. She apologises
  throughout, and she asks — repeatedly, reasonably — for *one*.

*Scaling: 3 PCs → HP 70. 4 → HP 85. 5 → HP 95. 6 → HP 110.*

### THE OTHER PILLAR — how to run the reveal

**Corrected timing (Change #18): the trigger is Hesper's first turn, not the
Roper's own initiative.** Don't roll initiative for the Roper at all. It sits
in the room exactly as described above — one of two identical pillars,
motionless, indistinguishable from its twin, its False Appearance doing the
work — and it stays completely inert through the whole fight **until Hesper's
first turn comes up on the tracker.** That's the cue, not a die roll. The
party gets however many rounds of setup happen before initiative reaches her
the first time (usually none or one, since she's a named combatant who
should act early), and then, right as she's about to act:

**Run this as a scripted beat outside the normal flow of combat** — timed to
land right as Hesper is mid-apology, mid-plea, asking them for *one*, at the
very start of what would have been her turn:

> It happens between one exchange and the next, and nobody sees it start. One
> of the two pillars was never a pillar. Six arms come up out of the
> substrate at once — throat, both wrists, the hand with the chain in it,
> both ankles, and last, almost gently, across her mouth. She doesn't get
> time to be surprised. Where she goes down, the floor takes her — pulled
> level, pulled flat, the pale substrate closing over her the way you'd
> erase a line you never should have drawn. One breath. Her chain hits the
> floor where she was standing. That's all that's left of her.

**Hesper is dead. Remove her from the fight — no save, no roll, it isn't
negotiable.** The pillar that moved is a **Roper** (*Monster Manual*, CR 5,
printed, unmodified — see the Bestiary; mechanically its Tendril attacks,
narratively six pale arms), and it's a fully active combatant from its next
turn on, reaching for whoever's nearest. Its twin is exactly what it always
was: an inert pillar. Any surviving doppelganger copies keep fighting
normally alongside it. **Don't inflate its HP or run it any differently at
smaller party sizes** — with the copies supplying action economy alongside
it, the printed stat block is correctly tuned; see **ENCOUNTER MATH**,
below.

**The one way to stop this from ever happening, and the party will never
know they did:** if they reach **close the file** — answer *HOW BIG IS A
HOME?* honestly, out loud — at any point *before Hesper's first turn comes
up*, neither pillar ever moves. The survey closes, the equipment stops, and
whatever was about to happen to Hesper simply doesn't happen. Don't tell
them how close it was. That's the reward for reaching the good ending fast,
and it should stay invisible.

### Endings

**Change #19, stated plainly: once the Roper and any remaining copies are
down, there is no further puzzle standing between the party and the door.**
Hesper already showed them the exit; they already understand the count/list
mechanics from her, the moment she explained the arithmetic. What happens
next is a concrete, practical procedure — not a second philosophical gate —
and *HOW BIG IS A HOME?* is never required to leave, only ever a way to end
things better than the fight already has.

**Run the close of combat as four plain steps, out loud, at the table:**

1. **Confirm who's standing.** Every PC, Ambry, and Cass (if rescued) — say
   the names.
2. **Count the open marks on the doorframe** against the people standing —
   see the arithmetic above. This is bookkeeping now, not a mystery; they
   already know why the numbers have to match.
3. **Check for anything that isn't who it says it is.** Ordinarily this is
   a non-issue — the doppelganger copies are visibly gone or visibly dead by
   this point, and nothing else in the finale room can pass for a person.
   Only if something is genuinely ambiguous (a copy that fled the fight
   rather than dying, say) does this step have teeth — see "The ugly one,"
   below, for what a real mistake here costs.
4. **Cross.**

**The swap** *(what happens if the party doesn't close the file)*. Hesper
doesn't leave — the other pillar saw to that. They walk out without her, and
the door doesn't care that they're one short; she was never on the list. It
costs them a person they'd started to like, and it isn't a choice they got
to make. That's deliberate, and it's a clean, complete ending on its own —
no further riddle required.

**Loot:** her chain is on her body — see [`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md).

**Close the file** *(the better ending, never required, and the only one
that saves her)*. If anyone has registered the question, they can answer it
instead of fighting — out loud, honestly, not cleverly. No roll. A Charisma
check can't buy this and shouldn't be offered; a bad-faith answer simply
doesn't land and the fight resumes. A true one closes the survey. The
equipment stops, and everything it was actively holding open collapses back
down to what it always really was underneath: the whole warped, folded space
around the party shrinks to an ordinary linen cupboard, with everyone
standing outside it, Hesper included, for about four seconds — she gets to
stand in an orchard, in wind, and then she doesn't. **Reached before Hesper's first
turn comes up, this also quietly cancels the reveal entirely** — see **THE
OTHER PILLAR**, above. Either way, once this happens, go straight to step 4
above: there's nothing left to solve, just a door to walk through.

**The ugly one** *(retained only as a concrete counting error, never as a
punishment for "failing" to answer the question)*. This should only ever
happen from an actual, adjudicated mistake at steps 2–3 above: the party
miscounts under pressure, or a fled doppelganger copy slips back into the
group unnoticed and crosses in place of someone it's impersonating. If you
use this ending, decide the specific mechanism before it happens — never
retroactively invent that "they didn't count carefully enough" after the
fact just because the scene needs drama. Discuss with your table in advance
whether this ending is in play at all; it can permanently replace a PC and
is considerably harsher than the rest of the adventure.

### Coming out

**Change #20: this is a real epilogue, not a paragraph — the Marchwell
family and the original writ both get a proper close before anything ends.**

**Through the pantry, into daylight.** The smell of apples going over. Oda
Marchwell in the doorway with her hands in her apron, and she has clearly
been standing there the entire time, however long that was on her side of
the door.

**If the real Cass came back with them:** give this room to breathe. Oda
doesn't manage words at first — she crosses the yard and simply holds onto
him. Cass, dry and a little embarrassed even now, deflects the moment almost
immediately with something practical: whether the pressing got finished,
whether anyone thought to feed the pigs, whether Oda let Tobin do too much
of the counting himself. That deflection *is* the reunion — he is
recognisably, entirely himself from the first word, which is the whole
payoff of rescuing him at all.

**If the real Cass was not rescued:** Oda has to be told what was living in
her kitchen for the past month, and that her actual brother may still be
somewhere in the Ell. Let her reaction be grief without collapse — she has
already suspected the worst since the moment "Cass" shut that door, and
being right is not the same as being ready. She doesn't blame the party. She
asks, once, whether there's any chance of going back for him, and accepts
"no" or "not today" without an argument, because she already knows the
answer.

**Tobin's beat.** Give him a small, human moment distinct from his optional
headcount — he was the first person in this family to survive the Ell and
come back, and nobody has ever quite known how to talk to him about it.
Something as simple as him quietly telling one PC, unprompted, that it does
get easier to stop counting eventually — even if that isn't entirely true —
does more work than a full scene. **Optional last beat:** he's in the
kitchen garden, and before anyone asks, he tells them how many of them there
are. He's counting correctly. Decide in the moment whether that's a comfort.

**Then the writ.** Surviving the Ell doesn't erase the reason they came.
Once the reunion has had its moment and the party has had a chance to eat,
rest properly, and let the silver get sorted (see "The prisoner and the
silver," below), **escort Ferris Ambry back to the tithe office that issued
the warrant** and turn him in. Collect the arrest fee (150gp, theirs
regardless) and the recovery fee (10% of whatever silver actually made it
out — see REWARDS.md) from the presiding tithe-official. This is a short,
low-stakes scene, and it should feel almost startlingly mundane after four
sessions in the Ell — deliberately so. **The legal and moral disposition of
Ambry himself is left entirely to the party;** some tables will turn him in
without a second thought, some will quietly let the paperwork be vague about
exactly how cooperative he was. Both are correct endings.

**And whatever they did, everyone who came out is slightly wrong, forever, in
a way that is never a mechanical penalty.** Each player picks or rolls one
from the "What You Came Back With" table in [`REWARDS.md`](REWARDS.md) —
that's the canonical copy; this beat is just the cue to run it.

**Closing shape of the whole adventure:** escape → Marchwell family
reunion/resolution → brief recovery → escort Ambry back → turn in the
fugitive and settle the fees. Nothing about the original mundane warrant job
gets skipped just because something ancient and impossible happened on the
way to finishing it.

## THE PRISONER AND THE SILVER

Two logistics questions your table will ask in the first ten minutes of
session 2. Both have answers, and both are better as pressure than as
bookkeeping.

### Ferris Ambry, prisoner

He is under arrest and he does not care, because **there is nowhere to run
to.** He does not need watching, roping, or manacling — he will not leave the
party's light, and if anyone walks off he follows at a trot. Treat him as
baggage that talks.

- **Mechanically:** noncombatant, commoner-like, AC 10, HP 6. He has no
  useful proficiency except a very good head for figures, which matters
  exactly once — in the substrate, where he can read the numbers faster than
  anyone and will not stop telling them what they say.
- **He is a drain on purpose.** He panics, he asks questions, he needs to be
  pulled out of things. In the rule-rooms he's a full extra body to manage:
  in the unanimity room he has to be doing the same thing as everyone else,
  and he is bad at that.
- **He counts on the tally.** One scratch, cut about two hours before theirs
  — the length of the chase that got him here first (see "The chase,"
  Session 1).

**The quiet ending nobody plans for:** by session 4 he is a man they have
carried, fed and nearly died for, and the writ is a piece of paper from a
world that stopped making sense three days ago. He will not ask to be let go —
he's too beaten for that — but he will look at them on the lane outside, and
somebody at the table will say something. Let them. It costs nothing and it's
the best five minutes of the epilogue.

### The strongboxes

**One strongbox: a small iron-strapped lockbox, roughly a foot and a half by
a foot, ~35 lb loaded** — ~800gp in coin plus a compact set of chapel
plate (a chalice, a paten, a small processional cross; not bulky serving
trays), sized so a frightened, unathletic clerk could actually grab it
one-armed on his way out the door and sling it across a saddle without
falling off his stolen horse (see "The chase," Session 1). Six characters
can carry that without anyone hitting encumbrance, so don't pretend it's a
load problem — it isn't, and players hate fake weight.

Make it a **decision** problem instead, three times:

1. **Session 1, the abandoned box.** They find it before they find him. It
   tells them how long he's been down here (weeks) when they've been down here
   an hour. Whether they pick it up now or come back for it is the first time
   the Ell teaches them that *coming back for it* is not a thing you can
   reliably do.
2. **Caching it.** Sensible, and they will. It works **only if they've mastered
   the fold rule** — a cache in an unmarked room is gone forever, and a cache
   in a room they've scratched a unique mark into is findable. This is the
   single best reward for engaging with the mechanic and you should let it pay
   off completely.
3. **The Hanging room (depth 4).** Nothing can be put down. Whatever they are
   carrying when they walk in, they are carrying until they leave — or they
   let go of it and it hangs there at that height forever, in a room they will
   never find again. Making a party choose between 800 gold pieces and free
   hands, out loud, is worth more than the gold.

**If they lose it:** the recovery fee is gone, the arrest fee isn't, and Oda
Marchwell has a brother and a barn full of cider and she is not ungrateful.

## EVERY QUESTION, ANSWERED

Nothing in this adventure is left open. If a player asks, there is an answer,
and you should give it — either in play through the substrate and Hesper, or
flat out at the table afterwards.

*Revised for Change #1's new ontology (Veyr, the Echo, and the Measure —
see `PREMISE.md` and the campaign canon files it links to). A few of these
answers are now genuinely uncertain even to Hesper — say so plainly where
noted rather than inventing false certainty.*

| They ask | The answer |
|---|---|
| Where did the chalk arrows come from? | Them. The equipment measures everything in the Ell, and the space itself never stops remaking itself on its own, folding what's been measured back into every copy — so their own marks get filed and echoed ahead of them, with nothing having to answer for it. |
| And the voices? | Same mechanism, on sound. Everything ever said in the Ell is measured, and the space's own ceaseless changing folds it back into the drafts, the same way it folds in everything else it was ever shown. |
| Then what was the oblex? | Not a captive, not lost — **native.** It belongs to the Echo itself, the domain this plot was staked out of, the way a gull belongs at sea. It found this file a long time ago and learned to live off what's recorded in it, which is why its duplicates spoke in voices and phrasings it had no other way to know. It was reading the file, not writing it. |
| Who built this? | **Nobody built the Ell — it's a plot of a god's domain, and the god was here first.** A mortal order, now extinct, called the Measure staked out that plot to survey it, and built the equipment doing the actual measuring: chains, drafting engines, reconstruction apparatus. Surveyors, not mystics — no theology, no bargain, no theory of immortality. Nobody currently alive knows their name unless they find it written down — see the Cutter's Rest and the reconstruction station. |
| Do we meet them? Is there someone to fight? | **No, and there never was.** The Measure died out long before anyone at the table was born. Nothing in the adventure is their agent, their ghost, or their servant — what the party fights is either the Ell's own machinery (which has no mind) or things that were already here before the Measure ever arrived (which have their own agenda, not the Measure's). |
| Why is the equipment still running if its builders are gone? | Because only the order that built it could formally close its survey — declare it finished and power it down. The Measure didn't survive long enough to close most of what it started. Nobody inherited the authority to switch it off, and the equipment has no protocol for stopping on its own. It's simply still doing its last assigned job. |
| Did it — or they — have a mind? | The Measure certainly did; they were people, and their motive was mundane, not mystical — they measured things because that's what they did, and this was simply the biggest thing they ever found to measure. **The Ell itself doesn't have a mind** — it runs on standing rules, not intentions. Installing draft-Cass wasn't a decision about *this* party; it's what the equipment automatically does whenever a house has a resident, because a resident who opens doors generates more to measure. It would have done the same for any family in any house. |
| Do we ever *see* the equipment? | **Not as one central machine — it's scattered, the way a survey crew's tools are scattered across a site.** The measured space itself carries a lot of it built in, the same way a tally is not separate from the wall it's cut into. But the party *can* see a distinct, working piece of it: the reconstruction station, depth 5, is the closest thing to "seeing the machine" the adventure offers. |
| Then how do we know it's real? | **They watch it work at least three times.** (1) A scratch cuts itself into the doorframe as the last of them crosses, in the first hour of session 1. (2) Their chalk marks are reproduced ahead of them, all session 2. (3) At depth 5 they watch a reconstruction station rebuild a version of the Marchwell kitchen from scratch, badly, then less badly — see "The reconstruction station," Session 4. |
| What does the Ell want? | To finish the survey. Nothing else. It is not malicious, not aware in any way that matters, and has never once registered a person as anything but a quantity. |
| Why can't it finish? | It cannot close the file on a *home*, because a home is not a volume — it is a thing that happens inside one, and the Measure's instrument has no unit for that. Hence the question in the substrate: **HOW BIG IS A HOME?** — the Measure's own unanswered research question, not a god's. |
| Why the anchor in the bedroom? Why the gallows? | It has seen those objects. It files them under *things that are in places*. There is no meaning and there never was — this is the one answer players find hardest to accept and it is the truth. |
| Why does the pantry door only work one way? | It isn't the same door working two ways — it's two different doors sharing a frame. The pantry has never had an exterior door; the interior one just stopped requiring anywhere sensible to lead. See "The pantry (the set-piece)," Session 1. |
| What happened to Tobin? | Nothing supernatural. He spent four days inside a place that counts everything and came out with the habit. He is a boy, he is himself, and he will be counting things for the rest of his life. |
| Who wrote "TRUST NO ONE" in his notebook? | Hesper, if the party asks her and shows her the notebook — see "Hesper Wick," Session 3. She found him partway through his wander, pointed him at a fold home, and left the one line she had time for, in scraped substrate-ash for ink. Optional; a genuinely satisfying answer if anyone thinks to ask, never required. |
| Why was it three weeks for Ambry and four hours for us? | Duration is a measured quantity like any other, and the instrument records it inconsistently between drafts. Nobody in the Ell can tell you how long they've been in it. |
| Who are the polite people at the dinner table? | Test residents. The equipment recorded, long ago, that this room needed occupants to check whether it worked, and the space's own ceaseless remaking keeps producing them on its own, unprompted, the way it produces everything else it was ever pointed at. They were never anyone and there is nothing to rescue. |
| What are the followers, really? | Something native to the Echo, the domain this plot was staked out of, cataloguing the party the way the oblex catalogued Ambry — patient, undirected, and not remotely stoppable by killing the one currently visible. Nobody in the adventure, Hesper included, can tell the party why they're interested, only that they are. |
| Who or what is "Veyr"? | A name spoken once, by a dying duplicate at the S2 midpoint, and otherwise never explained in play. If the party digs (the Cutter's Rest, the oblex, the reconstruction station's carved fragment) they'll find a mark, not a name — but the mark itself does explain something, if they hold the Twice-Cut Token up against the matching carving at the reconstruction station: it isn't a face or a signature, it's the fold rule drawn as a picture of itself, two identical things that are one thing. That's the closest the adventure gets to explaining what Veyr *is* without ever saying so. This is deliberate — see the revelation ladder, before Session 1. It's real campaign canon (`03_canon/GODS_AND_FAITHS.md`) if you ever want the full answer for your own prep, but the adventure itself never hands the name to the party outright. |
| Why can't Hesper leave? | The tally. The door gives back exactly the count it took. She came in through a different house, by a door that no longer exists, and has never been on this one's list. |
| Did anyone ever get out? | Yes — anyone on the list who walked back to their own door. Most people never find it again. The coats in the closet belonged to the ones who didn't. |
| Why is the light like that? | The instrument recorded illumination as a property of a space rather than something with a source, so the drafts are lit and there is nothing lighting them. |
| Has this happened to Marchwell before — is this an old haunting? | **No, and that's deliberate.** The instrument itself is ancient, but it doesn't sit on one subject forever — once it's drawn what it can from a place, it drifts and settles somewhere new. It only arrived at Marchwell within the last year; Tobin's midsummer disappearance was its first taste of this house, not its hundredth. That's why nobody here has any inherited caution about it — Oda is improvising, not remembering. |
| What happens to the equipment after we leave? | **Swap ending:** it keeps running, and — the same way it started on Marchwell only recently — in a few years it re-points itself and a farmhouse in the next valley grows a wing. **Close-the-file ending:** the survey completes and the equipment stops, for good, everywhere. |
| What was the thing that killed Hesper? | A Roper — an ordinary predator, not a device or a plan, and nothing to do with the Measure or Veyr either. It doesn't know who Hesper is. It's simply the kind of thing that lives in old, undisturbed measured space, and it had been sitting there, unnoticed, since before the party arrived. Its presence isn't a message. |

## ENCOUNTER MATH — 2024 DMG XP BUDGETS (6 PCs)

**Method:** 2024-style XP budget — per-character Low/Moderate/High × party
size, monster XP summed with **no group-size multiplier** (the 2024 DMG
dropped it). Six level-5 PCs: **Low 3,000 / Moderate 4,500 / High 6,600.**
Six level-6 PCs: **Low 3,600 / Moderate 6,000 / High 8,400.**

**Read the verdicts with two caveats, stated once:** the 2024 budget system is
honest about *attrition* but blind to *action economy*, and it cuts both
ways. Several attackers with Surprise Attack in a room with no exit hit much
harder than their summed XP implies — every "under Low" verdict below still
means "genuinely dangerous if mishandled," not "safe to ignore." A single
strong monster has the opposite problem: it only gets one turn against the
party's six, so a high-XP solo can die faster than its budget number
suggests (see the oblex, below). Trust the design notes over the raw number
either way.

| Encounter | Monsters (6 PCs) | Summed XP | vs. budget | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Four Casses (L5) | 4 × Doppelganger | 2,800 | Under Low (3,000) | Reads easy on paper; plays harder — Surprise Attack, four-way action economy, no exit from the room. **Correct as written.** |
| Coat Closet (L5) | 4 × Rug of smothering | 1,800 | Under Low | Correctly easy — a texture fight, not a threat. |
| Squat mimics (L5) | 3 × Mimic | 1,350 | Under Low | Correctly easy — the tension is which door, not the numbers. |
| Late cats, 2 displacers (L6) | 2 × Displacer beast | 1,400 | Under Low (3,600) | Easy and avoidable by design. Two beasts instead of one so it isn't a non-event if the party fights it. |
| Still bears, 3 owlbears (L6) | 3 × Owlbear | 2,100 | Under Low | Same logic — avoidable, and a real fight if they don't avoid it. |
| **Elder oblex (L5)** | 1 × Elder oblex, printed | 5,900 | Just under High (6,600) | **The opposite problem from the group fights above.** By raw budget this reads *near-deadly* — but it's a solo monster against six action economies, so it plays far easier than 5,900 XP implies: one turn a round against six is why the printed 115 HP dies in ~2 rounds regardless of its high CR. **See the homebrew note above** — HP is pushed further past printed to compensate for exactly this asymmetry. Target: **3–4 rounds** of the party's average output before it goes down. |
| **Hesper + up to 5 copies (L6)** | Hesper (custom) + 5 × Doppelganger | Copies alone: 3,500 | Copies alone under Low (3,600) | Hesper isn't a raw CR 3 veteran (see her profile) so she isn't budgeted; judge this half of the fight by rounds-to-clear, not adjusted XP. She's removed from combat by the Roper reveal (see **THE FOURTH RIDGE**) before it usually matters. |
| **Roper + surviving copies (L6)** | 1 × Roper, printed + up to 5 × Doppelganger | 1,800 + up to 3,500 | Under Moderate (6,000) at full copies | **Correctly tuned as printed — do not inflate the Roper's HP.** Unlike the oblex, it isn't fighting alone: up to six simultaneous enemy actors (Roper + 5 copies) gives it the action economy the oblex was missing, so the raw "under Moderate" budget already plays close to its true difficulty rather than under it. |

### Staggering the finale's copies

Bringing all five copies in on round one is a real alpha-strike risk against
six level-6 characters even though the raw XP reads low — five extra bodies
acting on turn one is exactly the action-economy blind spot the caveat above
is about.

**Bring the copies in one per round from round 2**, each stepping out of a
different doorway, independent of when the Roper's secret initiative lands.
Same total threat, spread across the fight, and the pressure *builds* as the
fight goes on rather than spiking all at once.

**If a table is struggling:** the copies are not required to die. They stop
when the Roper does.

## PARTY SIZE SCALING

**Level never changes — 5, then 6 after the midpoint.** Adjust bodies only.

| Oblex Eat Memories | alt. rounds | alt. rounds | printed | printed |
| Hesper HP | 70 | 85 | 95 | 110 |
| Roper HP (the other pillar) | 93 (printed, no scaling) | 93 | 93 | 93 |
| Minor encounters/session | 1 | 1–2 | 2 | 2 |
| Depth 3 rooms visited (of 12) | 6 | 6–8 | 8–10 | 8–10 |
| Rule-rooms per session 3 (of 6) | 3 | 4 | 4 | 5 |
| **Party copies** (S4) | cap 3 | cap 4 | cap 5 | cap 5 |
| Avoidable fights (S3) | 1 of 2, single creature | 1 of 2, single creature | both, full counts | both, full counts |

**Party copies are one per long rest taken, capped at the value in the table
above — whichever is lower, the flat cap of 5 or the party-size cap.** Past
the cap, add +15 HP to Hesper per extra rest instead. At three characters the
cap drops because three PCs plus Ambry plus Hesper plus three copies is
already a crowded, long fight.
