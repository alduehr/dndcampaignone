# Future One-Shot Ideas

Non-canon backlog — see [`README.md`](README.md). Nothing here is written yet.
When an idea gets built, move it out of this file into its own folder and add
it to the README table.

**Format used so far:** a concrete, one-sentence premise; one setting or
mechanic that makes it different from the last one; and a clock, a map, or a
structure that makes it runnable in a single sitting. Each idea should name
what the *engine* is, not just the monster.

## Queued (the user wants these)

### 1. The Heist

**Premise:** The party has until the shift change to get into a guarded vault,
take one specific thing, and get out.

**Engine:** Planning, then execution under a clock — the inverse of
*Highwater*. The party chooses *how*; the dungeon does not choose for them.

**What makes it different:**
- A short **planning phase** where players can scout, bribe, forge, and case the
  place, and every choice becomes a concrete advantage or a known obstacle in
  the run.
- **Combat is possible but loud.** Every fight costs time and raises alarm.
  Stealth, disguise, and social bypass are the primary routes.
- **Wrenches** thrown at the plan mid-run: an unexpected guard change, a
  vault door that behaves differently than scouted, a rival crew on the same
  night, a mark who isn't what the party was told.

**Structure ideas:**
- A **Heat track** (alarm level) that rises on noise, failed checks, and
  bodies, and unlocks new guard behavior at each stage.
- A **timer** tied to the guard rotation (real-time or abstract).
- A multi-floor or multi-room **map** with at least two routes in and two out.
- A **twist target**: the object, person, or document is not what the patron
  said it was.

**Decisions to make before writing:**
- Setting (counting-house, noble house, temple treasury, Concord-era vault).
- Real-time clock or abstract Heat track, or both.
- Level and party size (assume 3–6, tuned for four, unless told otherwise).
- Whether the party is hired, coerced, or doing it for themselves.

**Tags:** infiltration, social, stealth, planning phase, map-heavy.

### 2. The Summoning / The Thing That Will Wipe You

**Premise:** Something is being brought into the world. Either the party stops
the ritual in time, or it arrives — and it is far stronger than they are.

**Engine:** A fight the party is **not supposed to win by hitting it.** The
adventure is about *not facing it*, delaying it, or finding the one way to end
or trap it. If the party does face it head-on, they probably die, and that is
a fair outcome of a clearly telegraphed risk.

**What makes it different:**
- The **creature is a known, visible threat from the start** — the players
  know what it is, roughly what it can do, and that it outclasses them.
- **Two phases:** a *prevention* phase (disrupt the ritual, break foci, kill
  or turn the summoners, buy time) and a *survival* phase (the creature is
  here; run, seal, bargain, or bind it).
- **Multiple win conditions:** disrupt the ritual, banish it, seal it, trade
  with it, or escape and warn the town. Killing it is the worst option and
  should be possible only with a real, costly gambit.

**Design rules for a "will probably wipe the party" encounter:**
- **Tell the table up front.** At session zero, say that one fight in this
  adventure is not balanced and that fleeing, stalling, and clever plans are
  expected. This is what keeps it fair.
- **Telegraph it early and often.** Ruined villages, dead guards, a shadow on
  the horizon, a rival party's remains, a dying cultist's warning.
- **Give it rules the party can learn:** a weakness, a binding condition, a
  turn limit, a boundary it cannot cross, something it wants more than it wants
  to kill them.
- **Make the fight itself a clock.** The creature has a fixed number of
  rounds before it finishes something, or the ritual completes on a count.
- **Escape must be real.** There is always a way out — but it costs something
  (a bridge, an NPC, a relic, a town).
- **Death should be a story, not a cheap shot.** Plan for a possible TPK and
  include a short "what happens next" if everyone falls or flees.

**Variants to consider:**
- **A — Stop the ritual:** a dungeon crawl up to the summoning circle, with
  the creature arriving at a fixed time.
- **B — Face the thing that already came:** the creature is loose; the party
  must contain it, lure it, or hold a line for a set time.
- **C — Siege:** a fortified place, a count of waves, and a creature at the
  end of them.

**Decisions to make before writing:**
- Creature: what is it, what does it want, what are its rules?
- Variant (A, B, or C) or a hybrid.
- Real-time clock, abstract clock, or both.
- Level and party size.
- Tone: dread, tragedy, desperate heroism.

**Tags:** boss-you-can't-beat, ritual, countdown, flee-or-bind, high-lethality.

### 3. The Pirate Voyage (full sailing one-shot)

**Premise:** The party crews, steals, or inherits a ship and has one voyage to
reach a prize before a rival does. Sailing is the main mode of play, not the
transport between scenes.

**Engine:** The **ship is a character-sized resource** the whole party runs
together. Wind, heading, speed, repairs, and morale are things players manage
turn by turn; port, island, and boarding scenes are the payoff.

**Where it sits in Orrun:** The south and west coasts have the right
geography: the **Hollow Gulf Ports** (rival port city-states and licensed
raiders), the **Wracking Isles / Far Wrack** (a far-west island chain), the
**Calm Reach**, and the **Pale Sea**. Use the public regional files in
`locations/vael/orrun/` and invent every port, ship, and captain (see the
README's non-canon rules).

**What makes it different:**
- A **ship sheet** (hull, rigging, crew quality, morale, supplies, cargo)
  built on plain 5e: ship AC and HP, crew as a pool, repairs as skill checks.
  No new RPG system; it sits on top of 5e like the rest of the project.
- **Stations** each round at sea: helm, rigging, lookout, gunnery,
  damage control, boarding party. Every character has a real job, so six
  players are never spectators.
- A **wind and heading** mechanic (a small fixed table or a dial the DM turns)
  that makes tacking, pursuit, and running aground into decisions.
- A **sea map** with 6–8 named locations (a harbor, a reef, a storm band, a
  smugglers' cove, a wreck, an island, the rival's anchorage) and 2–3 routes.
- **Set-piece encounters:** a chase, a storm, a ship-to-ship fight ending in a
  boarding action, a reef navigation puzzle, and a port scene with a social
  problem.

**Structure options:**
- **A — The Race:** a rival crew heads for the same prize; each leg is a
  decision about speed, risk, and supplies. Ends at an island dungeon and a
  final boarding fight.
- **B — The Mutiny:** the party is the crew of someone else's ship, and the
  voyage is about who gets to run it by the end.
- **C — The Hunt:** a famous prize ship (or a sea creature) is out there;
  the party must find it, take it, and get away.
- **D — The Treasure Map:** a clue-driven voyage across several islands.

**Decisions to make before writing:**
- Structure (A–D) and the prize.
- Level and party size (assume 3–6, tuned for four unless told otherwise).
- How crunchy the ship rules should be: a one-page sheet, or a full
  chase-and-combat subsystem.
- Real-time clock, abstract clock (days of supplies, turns of wind), or none.
- Whether the party owns the ship or is hired onto it.
- Tone: swashbuckling, grim, supernatural, or comic.

**Tags:** ships, travel-as-play, boarding combat, sea map, crew roles, chase.

## Notes For Future Authors

- **Check the README's rules first:** one-shots are non-canon and invent their
  own disposable cast; they do not use registered campaign NPCs, factions,
  artifacts, or settlements by name. Public Orrun geography and culture are fair
  game (see `locations/vael/orrun/`).
- **Reuse the file pattern:** a main adventure file, a `MAP_SPEC.md`, a
  `PARTY_AND_LEVEL_SCALING.md`, and (for timer-driven ones) a `PACING_SHEET.md`.
  *Highwater* is the most complete current template.
- **Vary the engine,** not just the setting. Two puzzle caves in a row is one
  too many.
