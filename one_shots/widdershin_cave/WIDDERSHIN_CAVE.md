# Widdershin Cave

**A standalone one-shot for 3–6 characters of levels 1–4. Non-canon — see
[`../README.md`](../README.md).**

> "The sea's contrary. Ask it a thing straight on and it'll say no. Ask it
> widdershins and sometimes it forgets to."
> — Old Ebb Tarn

---

## THE BONES

**Premise:** A generation ago, smuggler captain Perrin Locke and his crew
stole a carved idol from something that lived beneath the waves, and the sea
took the whole crew as payment. Villagers say their ghosts still haunt the
sea-cave under Widdershin Point, and that the captain's treasure is still
down there for anyone brave — or foolish — enough to time the tide right.
The truth: Locke was trying to *return* the idol when the storm hit. His
crew still serve him, not out of malice, but because none of them have
figured out how to stop. And Locke still has the idol on his belt, because
in thirty years he has never once managed to let go of it.

**Where:** A headland cove on Orrun's Pale Coast — the western sea. Written
to sit a few miles down the coast from Cobble Strand, but it needs nothing
from that settlement's own canon; drop it near any small fishing village on
any west-facing coastline you're running. (If you move it to an *east*-facing
coast, flip the wind answer in the Zone 5 puzzle from west to east.)

**Party:** 3–6 characters, **tuned for four.** Levels 1–4; level 3 is the
sweet spot for a group one-shot, since everyone has a subclass and feels
capable. Full size × level scaling is in
[`PARTY_AND_LEVEL_SCALING.md`](PARTY_AND_LEVEL_SCALING.md). The numbers
printed in this file are the **baseline: four characters at level 1.**

**Characters:** Pregens or freshly built PCs both work. Assume standard
starting equipment for the level. At least one character with a bludgeoning
weapon or a damaging cantrip makes Zone 4 easier — the adventure telegraphs
this in Zone 3 rather than assuming it.

**Playtime:** ~3–3.5 hours for 3–4 characters; **~4–4.5 hours for 5–6.**

**The shape of it:**

1. **The village (10–15 min)** — pick up the legend, the practical problem
   (the tide), and the widdershins superstition from two or three fresh,
   disposable NPCs.
2. **Getting in (15–20 min)** — a real logistics puzzle: time the tide, or
   find the harder alternate route down the cliff.
3. **The cave, zones 2–5 (90–110 min)** — tide-pool hazard, a wreck full of
   atmosphere and a friendly ghost, a skeleton-crew fight at a choke point
   that spills into the open, and a three-part tide-dial puzzle with three
   independent clue paths.
4. **The boss, zone 6 (30–40 min)** — Captain Locke, playable as a straight
   fight, a puzzle-first narrative resolution, or a hybrid of both.
5. **Getting out / aftermath (10–15 min)** — the tide has closed the arch
   behind them, and the ending changes based on what they did with the idol.

**There is always a fight.** What changes is what the party is fighting
*for*. Locke's body is not entirely his own any more, and nothing anyone says
to him stops his arm coming up — he apologizes while he swings. The idol
cannot be prised off him, given away, or destroyed until he's been beaten
down past halfway, so no amount of cleverness skips the encounter.

**The two ways it ends**, at a glance:
- **Kill him.** Straightforward, achievable, and the *lesser* ending — the
  idol stays what it is and the crew stays stuck. This is the easy road.
- **Free him.** Get the Fathom Eye off him, then hold it against the empty
  altar for three rounds while he tries to stop you and begs you not to. The
  hard road, the better ending, and the actual climax of the adventure.

The peaceful resolution is a **combat objective**, not a bypass. You have to
fight him. You never have to kill him.

## THE MEAT

### The Village

Two or three short scenes are enough — don't overbuild this part, it's a
hook, not the adventure. Split the scenes across characters so one player
isn't carrying the whole hook.

- **Old Ebb Tarn**, a retired fisherman with a bad hip and a good memory. He
  actually watched the *Widdershin* go down as a boy. He's not superstitious
  about the cave so much as *respectful* of it — "the sea keeps what it's
  owed, and it was owed Locke's crew, and it took 'em fair." He does **not**
  know about the idol or the true history — just the legend. He offers three
  useful things:
  - He'll draw a rough tide-timing chart if asked (see **Getting In**).
  - "There's an old climbing path down past the lighthouse, if you don't
    fancy swimming for it."
  - **The widdershins superstition.** If anyone asks why the Point is called
    that: the old crews rowed widdershins — counterclockwise — around the
    headland for luck, and wound their nets and their capstans the same way
    when they wanted a thing to go well. "The sea's contrary. You answer it
    contrary." *This is a hint for the Zone 5 dials.* Don't push it; just say
    it and let it sit.
- **Young Sennow**, a fisherman's kid who dared a friend to swim into the
  cave mouth last summer. The friend came back with a fever and swore they
  heard someone crying. Sennow is a decent source of the "ghosts, not
  monsters" framing — useful for softening expectations before the fight.
  Sennow will offer to guide the party and **loses their nerve at the arch**
  regardless of what anyone says; they'll wait on the rocks with the party's
  spare lantern and be very relieved to see everyone again. Do not take a
  child into the cave.
- **Widow Mera Cask**, who runs the one inn in town, offers the job: a
  merchant paid her in advance, oddly generously, to find someone willing to
  retrieve "anything valuable" from the wreck, no questions asked. **The
  answer, if the party ever chases it:** the merchant is Locke's
  granddaughter, who has spent her adult life buying back a family name the
  coast still spits at, and who wants proof her grandfather wasn't a thief.
  She is right. That pays off nicely in the aftermath if the party returns
  the idol — hand her the truth instead of the treasure and she'll weep and
  double the fee.

None of these three need stat blocks. If violence somehow reaches the
village, treat them as ordinary commoners (AC 10, HP 4, no meaningful
attack).

### Getting In — The Tide Puzzle

This is the adventure's first real puzzle, and it's a logistics one, not a
riddle: **the cave mouth is a half-submerged sea-arch that's only passable
on foot at dead-low tide** (roughly a 1-hour window). Get the timing wrong
and the direct entrance is chest-deep or fully submerged.

Set the low tide **an hour before dawn.** That puts a full moon over the
Point on the way in (which matters in Zone 5), gives the cave its dim
half-light, and means the tide is well up by the time they come out.

- **Old Ebb's tide chart** (if the party asked) gives the next low-tide
  window directly — the "easy" path, costing an hour or two of waiting or
  timing their arrival.
- **A DC 12 Survival or Nature check**, reading the water and the exposed
  rockline, works out the timing without Ebb's help.
- **The cliff route** bypasses the tide puzzle entirely: an old, overgrown
  fisherman's path down the headland near the lighthouse, ending in a
  rope-and-chimney climb into the cave from above (**DC 13 Athletics**, or
  automatic with a rope and an anchor point, which is obviously available to
  anyone who thinks to bring one). This route drops the party into **Zone 3**
  directly, skipping Zone 2's crabs — a reward for players who scout ahead or
  ask around, not a trap. **The rope stays where they left it**, which is
  also their guaranteed way out.
- **Getting the timing wrong** isn't a dead end — it just costs time (an
  uncomfortable, cold wade with disadvantage on the first check inside) or
  forces the cliff route. Never block entry outright.

**A note on the cave's shape:** the floor **rises** as you go inland. The
sea-arch is the low point and the only part of the cave the tide actually
closes; everything behind it sits above the high-water line. That's why
Zone 2 has standing pools (water trapped in the hollows by the spring tides,
which is how real tide pools work) while Zones 3 and 4 are bone dry and
thirty-year-old crates are still crates. Zone 6 is the exception — it's open
to the ocean on its far wall and always tidal.

### Zone 1 — Tidewrack Landing

*Light: dim (daylight or moonlight through the arch).*

The sea-arch entrance: barnacled stone, the tideline a sharp dark stain
overhead, littered with old wreckage — a snapped mast, a bleached ship's
wheel half-buried in sand. Cold, salt air, and, faintly, something that
might be singing, or might just be the wind through the arch.

No encounter here. It's the establishing beat — let the party poke around; a
**DC 10 Investigation** check turns up a bent brass **belt-buckle engraved
with a stylized eye** (foreshadowing the Fathom Eye, no mechanical effect).

**Dressing that does work later:** everything exposed here is weathered from
one direction — the seaward side. Rock scoured smooth, timber silvered, the
one surviving iron ring rusted through on its western face. On this coast
the weather comes in off the water, and the water is west. *This is a hint
for the Zone 5 puzzle's wind dial.*

### Zone 2 — The Tide Pools

*Light: dim (luminous algae).*

A wide, uneven cavern floor cut through with a dozen tide pools, some
ankle-deep, some chest-deep, glowing faintly green-blue with luminous algae.
Skippable via the cliff route (Zone 1 → Zone 3 directly).

**Encounter — reef crabs (5 at baseline):** *Giant-crab-like* profile. AC
15 (natural armor), HP 6 each, Speed 20 ft (swim 20 ft). **Str +1, Dex +0,
Con +1, Int −4, Wis +0, Cha −3.** Claw +3 to hit, 1d4+1 piercing; on a hit
the target is **grappled (escape DC 11)**, and a grappling crab will drag its
catch toward the nearest chest-deep pool. **CR 1/8 each — low threat, fully
telegraphed** by clicking claws and rippling water before they attack, and
fully avoidable with a **DC 11 Stealth or Acrobatics** check to cross via the
drier stone spine running through the room. Fun, not scary — this is the
warm-up, and the drag-you-into-the-water bit is the joke, not a drowning
mechanic. A grappled character in a deep pool can escape or stand at the cost
of their action; nobody dies here.

**Treasure:** a corroded iron strongbox half-buried in the largest pool
(**DC 10 Strength to pry open**) holds 22 gp in mixed coin and
**Scrimshaw Fragment #1** (see **The Rune-Dial Vault**, below).

### Zone 3 — The Wreck Gallery

*Light: dim (bioluminescent fungus).*

The tide pools give way to a wide, high-ceilinged chamber where the actual
wreck of the *Widdershin* rests, canted against the cave wall, half-buried
in sand and glowing fungus that casts the whole room in soft blue-green
light. Genuinely beautiful, and the first place the adventure asks the
players to feel something other than caution.

**NPC — Tam, the drowned cabin-boy:** a translucent, barefoot boy of about
twelve, sitting on the wreck's broken rail, swinging his legs like he's still
waiting for someone. He's shy, a little embarrassed to be seen crying, and
immediately, disarmingly friendly if nobody attacks on sight (he can't hurt
anyone and won't try). **Tam knows the true story** — that Locke was trying
to give the idol back, that the storm caught them mid-ritual, that the crew
stayed loyal even in death because none of them blame him, and that the
captain has never been able to open his hand. He'll share all of it if asked
kindly, and will flatly **give away the tide-dial answer** (see below) if the
party is warm to him — there is no roll gate on kindness working. He's
frightened of the captain's grief, not the captain himself, and begs them not
to hurt him "if there's any other way."

**What Tam does not know:** how to actually free him. He's a twelve-year-old
who has watched his captain fail at the same thing for thirty years and has no
idea why it doesn't work. He can tell the party *what* Locke wants; he cannot
tell them how to give it to him, and he will say so, miserably, if asked.
Don't let him shortcut the climax — that's the fight's job.

**If someone attacks Tam:** the blow passes through him and he is simply
gone — no attack roll, no damage, no combat. He does not come back, the
Zone 5 bypass closes, and the room gets very quiet. That's the whole
consequence; don't punish it further.

**What Tam wants (optional):** he lost his lucky knucklebone in a dice game
with the bosun the week before the wreck and never won it back. It's in the
Zone 4 cache. Bring it to him and he presses it into a living hand — see
**Tam's Knucklebone** under rewards.

**Tam in the boss fight:** if Tam was treated well, he'll appear in Zone 6
and can use **one round of distraction** — see Locke's stat block.

**Dressing that does work later:** among the wreck timbers lie the smashed
remains of one of the crew — ribs staved in, skull cracked open like a nut —
and beside it a rust-pitted **boarding maul**. A **DC 10 Investigation** (or
just looking) makes the lesson plain: these things come apart under a heavy
blow far better than under an edge. The maul is a functional
**maul/warhammer** for anyone who wants it. *This telegraphs the skeletons'
bludgeoning vulnerability without making party composition a lottery.*

**Treasure:** **Scrimshaw Fragment #2**, tucked in the wreck's shattered
captain's locker.

### Zone 4 — The Smuggler's Cache

*Light: dark.*

A narrow, low tunnel opens into a cramped storeroom stacked with rotted crates
and barrels — the actual contraband cache. Most of it is thirty years past
saving, but not all, and one crate in here is not contraband at all (see
**What's in the crates**, below). The tunnel is **one person wide**, and
holding it is a genuinely good idea. It is not, however, the whole fight — see
deployment below.

**The crew at work.** Before anything notices the party, let them watch for
a moment. The drowned crew are still doing their jobs. One drags a rotted
holystone back and forth over a patch of stone floor, swabbing a deck that
burned to the waterline thirty years ago. One counts the same seven coins
into a stack, knocks the stack over, and counts them again. One stands at
attention beside a mast that isn't there, waiting for an order. **They are
not cruel. They are stuck.** This is the whole theme of the adventure in one
tableau, and it lands better than any amount of read-aloud.

They attack once noticed — not with malice, but because boarders are boarders
and someone has to repel them.

**Encounter — drowned skeleton crew (4 at baseline):**
*Skeleton-like, salt-crusted* profile. AC 13, HP 13 each, Speed 30 ft.
**Str +0, Dex +2, Con +1, Int −2, Wis −1, Cha −3.** Scimitar +3 to hit
(1d6+1 slashing) or shortbow +3 to hit at range (1d6 piercing). **Vulnerable
to bludgeoning; immune to poison damage and the poisoned and exhaustion
conditions; resistant to cold damage.** **CR 1/4 each.** Mindless and
relentless (no morale — they fight until destroyed), but **slow and clumsy
climbing**, so retreat back through the tunnel into Zone 3 is always
available and always effective.

**Deployment — this is the fix for "everyone stands in line."** Don't run
this as a queue:
- **One** skeleton holds the tunnel mouth and fights whoever comes.
- **Two** push out past it into Zone 3 within a round or two and fight in the
  open gallery — a big, already-mapped, beautifully lit room with a shipwreck
  in it. Now the chokepoint is a *tactic the party chooses* ("hold the line
  and let them come to us") rather than a bottleneck the map forces on them.
- **Any remaining** skeletons take the shortbows and shoot down the tunnel
  from inside the cache, which pulls the party's archers and casters into the
  fight from Zone 3 without anyone needing to squeeze forward.
- Crate and barrel clusters in the room give **half cover (+2 AC)** to
  whoever's using them, skeletons included.

**What's in the crates.** Players will poke at these within thirty seconds of
walking in, so have answers ready. Locke ran luxuries, not weapons — which is
itself a small argument about what kind of man he was.

| Container | Contents |
|---|---|
| Wax-sealed barrels | **Salt**, still perfectly good — salt doesn't rot. Worth **40–60 gp** to the right buyer, and see the note below. |
| Staved casks | **Brandy.** Most burst long ago; two or three are intact and drinkable. Someone will drink it. |
| Bolts of cloth | **Lace and silk**, rotted to a grey slime that comes apart in the hands. |
| Bales | **Untaxed wool**, now one felted, stinking mass. Nothing inside. It smells appalling and the smell gets on people. |
| Oilskin packets | **Tea and pepper**, ruined but unmistakable by smell. |

**The salt is a joke with teeth.** It's the most valuable ordinary thing in the
cave and it's in barrels, and the way out is a 60 ft swim through a flooded
sea-arch. Let them work that out themselves. A party determined to get it home
deserves to, somehow.

**The crate that isn't contraband.** Shoved apart from the others, newer than
the rest: a fitted wooden travelling-case, packed with straw, its lid unpegged.
It is **empty** — and the hollow pressed into the straw is unmistakably the
shape of a wide, staring eye, about the size of a fist.

Locke had the Fathom Eye *packed for a journey* the night he died. Nobody
crates up a thing they intend to keep. This is a fourth, entirely optional
route to the true story, and unlike the others it points at **motive** rather
than at the dial answer — a party that finds it walks into Zone 6 already
suspecting he wasn't a thief, which makes the parley land considerably harder.
No check required beyond opening the crate; a **DC 10 Investigation** connects
the hollow to the belt-buckle from Zone 1 if anyone wants confirmation.

**Treasure:** 35 gp in a strongbox; a mundane silver hip-flask (sellable,
~15 gp); **2 potions of healing** in an oilskin-lined medicine chest
(smugglers plan for knife fights); **Tam's knucklebone**, in a cup on an
upturned crate beside the coin-counting skeleton; and, pinned under a fallen
crate, a carved stone **tide-chart disc** — see below.

### Zone 5 — The Rune-Dial Vault

*Light: dark.*

A round chamber, obviously artificial rather than natural — smoothed walls, a
single heavy stone door on the far side carved with a wide, staring eye, and
set into the door: **three stone dials**, each ringed with eight symbols the
party will recognize as tide/moon/wind markers once they've seen the chart
from Zone 4.

**The dials only turn counterclockwise.** They're ratcheted — push one
clockwise and it simply won't go. To move a dial back one symbol you take it
all the way around the other way. This never blocks anyone; it's tactile,
it's the thing the Point is named for, and Old Ebb told them about it in the
village. *You answer the sea contrary.*

**The answer:** **full moon / dead slack tide / wind off the sea (west)** —
"the drowning tide," the exact conditions of the night the *Widdershin* went
down.

**The three dials are three separate deductions,** and the clues give
*constraints* rather than the whole answer, so the party actually reasons
their way in:

| Dial | What the clues give you |
|---|---|
| **Moon** | Scrimshaw #1: *"...the moon full and fat over the Point..."* The tide-chart disc also marks it. And they walked in under a full moon an hour ago. |
| **Tide** | Scrimshaw #2: *"...we went in on the slack..."* The disc's gouged, circled combination marks the killing tide. And they already know what dead-low tide means here — they used it to get in. |
| **Wind** | Scrimshaw #2: *"...against the wind off the sea, and never came..."* The disc's wind mark is worn illegible, so this one has to be reasoned: **on this coast the water is west.** Zone 1's weathering shows it, Old Ebb says it, and any sailor, fisher, or Nature/Survival check confirms it. |

Three independent routes to the whole thing, so no single find or roll gates
progress:

1. **The tide-chart disc (Zone 4)** — moon and tide directly; wind worn away.
2. **Scrimshaw Fragments #1 + #2 (Zones 2 and 3)** — moon, tide, and
   "wind off the sea," which the party converts to *west* using local
   knowledge. Either fragment alone still gives part of it.
3. **Tam (Zone 3)** — will just say all three out loud if asked kindly. The
   free, no-puzzle bypass for a table that wants less friction.

**Setting the dials wrong** triggers a harmless flooding jet from the door
itself (**DC 12 Dexterity save** or take 1d4 cold damage and get thoroughly
soaked) and resets all three dials to their start position — a cost, not a
dead end. There is no limit on attempts. Brute force isn't realistic (8³ =
512 combinations), so this is never a stall tactic.

Once solved, the eye carved into the door "blinks" open, and **it stays
open** — the way back is never closed behind the party.

Beyond the door, a short flight of worn steps leads **down** into Zone 6.
The vault sits above the high-water line; Zone 6 does not. That stair is why
this room has been dry for thirty years, and the party will feel the air
change on the way down.

### Zone 6 — The Widdershin Deep

*Light: dim (moonlight through the sea-arch, refracting off the water).*

The cave opens into a vast sea-cavern, its far wall open to the ocean itself
— waves roll in slowly through a low natural arch, and moonlight ripples
across black water. A wrecked ship's-chair, lashed with kelp and barnacles,
sits on a raised stone shelf above the water: Locke's "throne."

**Beside it, a low stone altar, worn smooth by centuries of tide — and
empty.** Swept clean. Something is supposed to be there, and everything in
this cave is arranged around the fact that it isn't.

It's on Locke's belt. It has been on Locke's belt for thirty years.

**Make sure the party sees the thread.** Wound through the links of the chain
that holds the Fathom Eye is a thin, slick line of black water, and it runs
off the shelf, down into the deep, and does not go slack when he moves. It is
the single most important detail in the room — it's why he can't let go, it's
why the idol won't come off until he's beaten down, and a party that *sees* it
will read the fight as a problem to solve instead of a wall. Describe it before
initiative if you possibly can.

This is both the treasure room and the boss lair — the fight and the loot are
in the same breath, which is the point.

**Boss — Captain Perrin Locke, the Widdershin Warden.** See full writeup
below.

**Treasure (regardless of the fight's outcome):**
- A locked sea-chest behind the throne (**DC 13 to pick, or the key is on
  Locke's belt**): **300 gp** at baseline in old coin and jewelry, a fine but
  mundane boarding cutlass worth 25 gp to a collector, and **2 Widdershin
  Coins** — genuine minor magic items (see below).
- **The Fathom Eye idol itself** — see **Resolution**, below.

## The Boss — Captain Perrin Locke

*A tragic figure, not a villain. Play him grieving and tired, not cruel — he
stopped the storm from taking anyone else the only way he knew how, and he's
been paying for it for thirty years. He is not guarding the idol. He is
carrying it, and he would give anything to be rid of it, and he cannot.*

**Chassis:** a Wight-like frame with Specter-like mobility, built custom
below — not a copy of any published block.

**Type:** Undead (unique). **Size:** Medium. **CR 2** (at the baseline line
below). **AC:** 13. **HP:** 40 (baseline: four characters at level 1 — see
[`PARTY_AND_LEVEL_SCALING.md`](PARTY_AND_LEVEL_SCALING.md) for every other
combination). HP does **not** change between phases; only his resistance
does. **Speed:** 30 ft, hover 20 ft — he moves freely over the water in
Zone 6 and is unaffected by his own Tide Call.

**Str +3, Dex +1, Con +2, Int +0, Wis +1, Cha +3.** Proficiency +2.
**Saves:** Wis +3, Cha +5. **Skills:** Perception +3, Intimidation +5.
**Senses:** darkvision 60 ft, passive Perception 13. **Languages:** the
common tongue of the coast.

**The Fathom Eye (why he can't just hand it over).** The idol hangs at his
belt on a corroded chain, and wound through the links is something that is not
metal — a slick black thread of water that runs down off the shelf and into
the deep, and does not slacken. Whatever he stole the Eye from is still down
there, still holding the other end, and it does not consider the debt settled.
That's what keeps his hand shut. It is not grief and it is not a choice.

He has been trying to put the thing back since the night he drowned. **A
living hand can do it.** He knows that. He will say so, plainly and with
enormous difficulty, to anyone who gives him the chance — and then he will
attack them anyway, because his arm is not entirely his own, and he will
apologize while he does it.

**Idol Resistance (Phase 1):** while the Fathom Eye is on Locke's person, he
is **resistant to bludgeoning, piercing, and slashing damage from nonmagical
weapons.**

**The half-HP gate — this is the important rule.** While Locke is **at or
above half his HP, the Fathom Eye cannot be removed by any means.** The thread
holds. Disarms slip, blades skate off the chain, the idol shrugs off damage,
and he cannot open his hand no matter how badly he wants to or how well the
party talks to him. Describe the failure honestly the first time someone tries
— *the chain doesn't so much resist as refuse* — so it reads as a puzzle-state,
not as the GM saying no.

**Below half HP,** the thread goes slack, and every route opens at once:
- **He gives it to you.** A free action on his turn, the moment he can. If
  the party has parleyed at all, he does this without being asked.
- **Disarm:** as an action in melee, a **DC 15 Strength (Athletics) or
  Dexterity (Sleight of Hand) check** to lift the idol off his belt. Failure
  by 5 or more gives him a free Cutlass attack in response.
- **Cut the chain:** an attack against AC 15 targeting the belt; a hit of any
  damage frees it and it drops at his feet.
- **Destroy it in place:** the idol is AC 12, 8 HP, immune to poison and
  psychic damage; a hard object plus a **DC 15 Strength** check also works.

**Phase 2** begins the moment the idol leaves him. His resistance ends, and he
visibly becomes more solid, more tired-looking, and easier to hit. **He does
not stop fighting.** The thread is off the idol now — it's in him.

### The altar — the real climax

Getting the Eye off Locke is not the ending. Putting it back is.

**The rite:** a character holding the Fathom Eye must **hold it against the
empty altar for three consecutive rounds.** It takes their action each round
and they cannot move off the altar's square. At the end of the third round the
debt is paid and the fight is over — see **Resolution**.

- **If the holder is moved, knocked prone, incapacitated, or spends their
  action on anything else,** progress drops back by one round rather than to
  zero. This is a grind, not a trap; it should feel like inches.
- **The idol can be handed off** — a new holder picks up where the last one
  left off. Passing it up a chain of characters while people fall over is
  exactly the kind of scene this is for.
- **Locke will attack the holder,** preferentially and relentlessly, while
  begging them not to stop. He does not choose this. Play both halves out
  loud; it's the best thirty seconds in the adventure.
- **A short table** (three characters, level 1) can drop this to two rounds if
  the fight is already running long. Don't drop it to one — the whole point is
  that the good ending has to be held onto.

Now the party has jobs: someone holds the altar, someone keeps Locke off them,
someone keeps the holder standing. That is what the shelf and the fifteen feet
between throne and altar were drawn for.

**Actions:**
- **Cutlass of Drowning.** Melee attack, +4 to hit, reach 5 ft: 1d8+2
  slashing plus 1d6 cold damage.
- **Tide Call** (uses his action; recharges on a 5 or 6 at the start of his
  turn, **capped at twice per fight** regardless of recharge rolls): the water
  in the room surges. Every creature in the open water must succeed on a
  **DC 13 Strength save** or be knocked prone and **pushed 10 ft toward the
  sea-arch.** Telegraph this clearly — the water visibly begins to churn on
  the round before — so it never reads as a cheap hit. (It does not add
  difficult terrain; the open water already is.)
- **Wailing Command** (bonus action, once per fight): if any of the drowned
  crew from Zone 4 are still standing, he calls them to his side — 2 rounds
  of travel time, giving fair warning. If they've all been destroyed, this is
  a mournful, echoing shout and nothing else. **Never manufacture new
  skeletons.**

**Tam's Distraction** (if Tam was treated kindly): once per fight, as a
reaction when Locke makes an attack, Tam calls out to him — *"Cap'n. Don't."*
Locke has **disadvantage on that attack roll**, and if it misses he does not
move for the rest of his turn. Locke never targets Tam and never will.

**Turn Undead.** Locke can be turned normally (Wis save +3). He does not
flee the cave — he withdraws to the far water, hovers there with his back to
the party, and waits it out. Treat a successful turn as buying the party a
round or two and a good look at how little he wants this, not as an escape.
The skeleton crew are turned normally with no special ruling.

**Tactics:** Locke fights from the water when he can (mobility, Tide Call
setup) and doesn't chase a retreating party out of Zone 6 — he's bound to
this cave and knows it. He fights defensively over the shelf if anyone goes
for the altar, not because he wants to stop them, but because thirty years of
guarding is a hard habit to break, and a part of him is afraid of what
happens when it's over.

**Parley — always available, and it never stops the fight.** At **any** point
a character can ask him to stop, ask what he wants, or ask about the altar:
**DC 14 Persuasion** (**DC 12 if Tam is present and vouching; DC 10 once he's
below half HP**). This window never closes.

What a success buys is **information, not peace** — he tells them the truth
about the thread, the altar, and the living hand, and it is the single most
useful thing that can happen in this room. What it does not buy is his weapon
going down, because that isn't his to offer. He talks *between swings*. A
party that opens with diplomacy gets the whole solution handed to them on
round one and still has to earn it, which is exactly the right trade.

Do not let a good roll here end the encounter. If a player expects it to, be
straight with them: *he wants what you want, and he cannot stop, and that is
the problem you're here to solve.*

**Danger rating:** hard, verging on deadly for four level-1 characters while
Idol Resistance is up; manageable once it's down, though the altar rite means
Phase 2 is spent defending someone who is standing still and not fighting
back. Budget for one character going down. The Zone 4 fight and the Zone 5
puzzle both exist partly to make sure the party arrives with resources and
information intact.

**Escape valves** (unchanged, and there are still three): retreat through the
open vault door, which he will not follow past; the parley, which converts a
blind fight into an informed one; and killing him, which always works and is
always available and is always the sadder ending.

### Resolution: what happens to the Fathom Eye

- **The rite completed on the altar** *(three rounds held; see above)*: the
  best ending, and the only one that has to be fought for. The black thread
  goes out of the water like a held breath let go. Locke's crew finally goes
  still — in Zone 4, a holystone stops mid-stroke. He thanks the party, sits
  back in his chair, tells them where the chest key is if they hadn't found
  it, and fades. Tam waves. The village later reports the tide "feels calmer"
  past Widdershin Point, and Mera Cask's merchant gets the truth she paid for.
- **Locke destroyed before the rite is finished:** the easy road. He goes
  down, apologizing, and does not come back. But nothing was returned — the
  Eye is still an idol with a thread on it, the crew is still swabbing a deck
  that burned thirty years ago, and the party leaves with treasure and an
  unfinished feeling. This is a legitimate ending and most tables that pick a
  fight will get it. **Do not editorialize.** Let the holystone still be
  scraping on the way out and let them notice it themselves.
- **The idol destroyed instead of returned:** same as above, plus the room's
  water surges once, hard (everyone present takes 1d6 cold damage, no save) as
  the thread snaps. Locke is released and fades. The crew is not.
- **Thrown into the sea-arch:** the instinctive move, and it half works. Locke
  is released — but the sea wanted the thing *returned*, not discarded, and
  the crew stays where it is. A real ending, just a lesser one; don't take it
  away from them, and don't pretend it was the good one.
- **Kept:** a striking curio worth ~100 gp to the right collector, and nothing
  bad happens for keeping it. Seed a follow-up rumor hook (an old sailor gets
  nervous around it, a storm seems to follow the ship that carries it) if you
  ever want a sequel. Locke's crew stays stuck, and so does he.

### Rewards

**The Widdershin Coin** *(minor magic item; 2 at baseline)*. A tarnished
silver coin stamped with the same staring eye as the idol. **Once per
adventure**, when the holder fails a Strength or Dexterity saving throw
against being knocked prone, moved, or swept off their feet, they can flip
the coin as a reaction to reroll the save. Small, thematic,
level-appropriate, and not remotely game-breaking.

**Tam's Knucklebone** *(minor magic item; only if earned)*. A small,
sea-smoothed knuckle of bone, warm to the touch. **Once per adventure**,
reroll one failed ability check. Tam will not take it back and will be
delighted, in a shy way, that someone wanted it.

**The boarding cutlass** — mundane, fine work, worth 25 gp to a collector.
Exactly one exists at every party size.

**Coin and consumables** scale with party size; see
[`PARTY_AND_LEVEL_SCALING.md`](PARTY_AND_LEVEL_SCALING.md).

### Getting out

The tide has come in behind them. This is a designed consequence, not a
surprise — the arch is a **60 ft swim** in cold water: **DC 12 Athletics**,
and a failure costs a dropped non-stowed item or a level of exhaustion,
never a death. Alternatives: wait roughly five hours in Zone 3 for the next
low water (Sennow will be frantic), or climb out on the cliff route's rope
if they came in that way or thought to rig it. Tam, if he's still around,
knows which pools connect to the outside and will happily point.

## Running Notes

- **If the party stalls on the tide-dial puzzle:** any one of the three clue
  routes resolves it, and Old Ebb's superstition covers the direction. If a
  table is visibly stuck despite having clues in hand, let Tam show up early
  (he can visit Zone 4 or 5 if they were kind to him) and just say the answer
  — never let a puzzle eat more than 15–20 real minutes.
- **If the party skips Zone 2 or 4 entirely** (cliff route, or clever
  avoidance): that's fine. Nothing there is required to reach or beat the
  boss — losing Scrimshaw #1 just means leaning harder on the disc or Tam.
  The three-route design means no single skip creates a dead end. They will
  miss the potions of healing and Tam's knucklebone, which is a real cost and
  a fair one.
- **If the party tries to talk their way out of the fight entirely:** they
  can't, and the reason is in the fiction, not in your authority. He agrees
  with them. He is trying to do what they're asking. His arm keeps coming up.
  Say it once clearly and then let the dice run — the tragedy is the whole
  point, and a player who understands *why* the fight is unavoidable will
  enjoy it more than one who was allowed to skip it.
- **If the party works out that they can just take the idol and leave:** yes,
  once he's below half HP — which means they fought him for it. Locke isn't
  freed, but they're alive and rich, and that's the "thrown into the sea"
  ending's cousin. Let them have it.
- **If the fight is running long,** cut the altar rite to two rounds, or let
  Locke's morale break under half (he stops pursuing the holder and only
  defends himself). Don't cut the half-HP gate — that's the thing keeping the
  climax from evaporating.
- **If a player finds a genuinely clever way to short-circuit the rite** —
  *dispel magic* on the thread, shoving Locke into the water and holding him
  under, dropping the idol into a *bag of holding* mid-swing — reward it. Cut
  the rite to one round, not to zero. They still have to plant it on the
  altar with him alive in the room; they just made it much easier, and that's
  what cleverness should buy.
- **If a character dies:** this is a one-shot; a death should land, not
  derail. Locke does not coup-de-grâce the unconscious, the skeletons do (they
  don't know any better — telegraph it), and the crabs cannot. If you want a
  softer table, rule that anyone who goes down in Zone 6 is dragged onto the
  shelf by the tide rather than lost in the water.
- **Levelling:** if you're running this as the start of something, one level
  at the end is right. Otherwise treat it as milestone-only and don't track
  XP.
- **Tone:** melancholy-adventurous, not horror. The skeletons are a fair
  fight and a sad joke, not a jump-scare; Tam and Locke are sad, not
  sinister. Save any real dread for the Tide Call going off unexpectedly.
