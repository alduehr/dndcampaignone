## Summary

Faith in Orrun centers on the **Three Thresholds** rather than a crowded pantheon: three divine principles governing Birth, Living, and Death. There is no grand church anymore — the Concord was the closest thing, and it fell. Today faith is fragmented between the Mourners' Circle (folk death-faith), threshold shrines, and scattered clergy. Clerics, paladins, and divine casters function normally in 5e terms; the gods are real but distant.

---

## The Three Thresholds (Player-Safe)

| Threshold | Domain | Symbol | Common Title |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Opening** | Birth, beginnings, the unborn, hope | An open ring | "the First Threshold" |
| **The Hearth** | Living, kinship, oaths, the everyday world | A flame within a square | "the Threshold We Stand In" |
| **The Last Door** | Death, memory, passage, the dead | A closed door with a single star | "the Last Threshold" |

- Most people honor all three at different life moments; few are exclusive devotees.
- 5e clerics/paladins draw from these as appropriate domains (Life, Light, Grave, War, Peace, etc. all fit under one Threshold or another).
- The **Last Door** is the most actively worshipped in the Reach because of the death-rite crisis.

## Clergy and Practice

- There is no unified church. Clergy are local: a village rite-speaker, a shrine-keeper, a wandering preacher.
- The **Mourners' Circle** (see factions) is the largest organized faith in the Reach — folk death-keepers, not a temple hierarchy.
- The **Ashen Wardens** are quasi-religious: rite-keepers who lay the restless dead. Many think of them as holy; they are technically an order, not a church.
- Divine magic works normally. A "failed rite" (the campaign's signature horror) is *not* a failure of the priest's faith — it is the world failing, which terrifies clergy who cannot explain it.

## Folk Beliefs (Player-Safe)

- "Salt at the sill, a star at the door" — common funeral protections against a Remembrance.
- The dead should be **named once and released**, never called back. Speaking a dead name three times "opens the door wrong" (folk fear of the Gravecallers).
- Mirrors are covered in a house of the recently dead so the dead "don't see a way to stay."

## DM-Only Truth About the Gods

- The Three Thresholds are **real but distant** divine principles. They do not micromanage the world and did not cause the Quietfall.
- The **Last Door is partly a mechanism the Concord tapped** (see `COSMOLOGY.md`). The gods did not sanction the harvest and are not its authors; the Concord exploited the Last Door without divine permission.
- No god in this setting is secretly the villain. The campaign's evil is **institutional and human** (the Concord/Hollow Court), which is thematically important: the horror is made by people, not gods.
- Clerics who pray for guidance about the crisis receive *grief and silence*, not answers — fuel for the mystery, not a shortcut to the truth.

## Minor and Foreign Powers

The Three Thresholds are the faith of Orrun. They are not the only real power in the cosmos — a handful of minor, fringe, or foreign gods are also real, also distant, and receive scattered worship without threatening the Thresholds' primacy or the "no crowded pantheon" rule. Add to this table sparingly, and only when a minor power earns its keep through actual play content.

| Name | Domain | Symbol | Worship |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Veyr** | Echoes, memory, repetition, the fear of being forgotten | A single word or mark, carved twice, identically, side by side | Fringe and scattered — surveyors, cartographers, archivists, record-keepers, and mourners anxious that their dead will fade from memory. No temples; small shrines and private household rites only. Not part of the Mourners' Circle or the Last Door faith, and the two are sometimes in quiet tension (see below). |

### Veyr, God of Echoes and Memory

Veyr is real, distant, and minor — a god the way a village shrine is a religion: true, but not what most people mean when they say "the gods." Veyr's worshippers believe that what is measured, repeated, or recorded precisely enough leaves an *echo* that outlasts the thing itself — a form of permanence that has nothing to do with the Last Door or the Quiet Country. A surveyor's field-book, a name spoken correctly at the right grave, a house drawn to the exact inch: these are small acts of devotion whether or not the person doing them knows Veyr's name.

**Folk practice:** some communities — mostly in old surveying and archive-keeping trades — say a dead person's name once more, precisely, some months after the funeral rites are done, "so it echoes kindly, rather than endlessly." This sits in quiet tension with Mourners' Circle practice (which holds that a name spoken three times "opens the door wrong") without contradicting it outright; the two folk traditions simply disagree about what a repeated name does, and neither side is provably right in front of the player.

**DM-only truth:** Veyr's domain is a real plane called **the Echo** (see `COSMOLOGY.md`) — not to be confused with **the Echo Realm**, which is an unrelated regional folk-name some Orrun cultures use for the Last Telling (see `locations/vael/orrun/timelines/the-last-telling`). The two are different cosmological systems that happen to share a word; never conflate them at the table. Veyr's domain and servants are the DM-only backbone of the ancient mortal order called **the Measure**, who staked out and surveyed plots of the Echo (called Ells) and left equipment behind still measuring them (see `DM_ONLY_CANON.md` and `one_shots/the_ell_at_marchwell/`). Veyr did not build the Echo for them, sanction their survey, or command the Measure — the same way the Three Thresholds did not sanction the Concord's harvest. Veyr's native servants are simply curious, patient, and still present wherever the Measure's leftover equipment keeps running.
