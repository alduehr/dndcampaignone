## AI Use

Load for travel and culture across the mid-continent chalk downs — the gentlest of the far regions and the natural crossroads of the south-central interior.

## One-Sentence Identity

Settled chalk-down sheep country and ancient barrow-fields: a gentle, orderly, custom-bound pastoral heartland of manorial holds and shire-moots.

## Geography

Rolling chalk grassland — open sheep-walks, hedged manor-fields, dry-stone folds, and beech hangers in the combes. The land is high, dry, and windy, with springs breaking out where the chalk meets clay.

Defining features:

- **The Barrow-Fields** — extensive ancient burial mounds on the eastern downs, far older than any road or law in the region. Every shepherd knows they must be left to lie.
- **The Penmark vale** — hedged manorial farmland to the west, the region's richest ground.
- **The wool-walks** — high grazing around the southeastern villages.
- **The Mardenflow headwaters** — the great southern river rises here among the downs and runs south toward the delta.
- **The moot-stone** — the standing stone at the shire-town where the region's assemblies are held; an institution as much as a rock.

**Borders:** the Glassmere League (north), the Saltmere Reaches (east), the Sallowmarch delta (south, down the Mardenflow), the Sunmark's warm forests (southwest).

## Climate

Dry and breezy, with cold clear winters and warm summers. The downs get less rain than the country around them and lose it fast through the chalk; water is a real consideration on the high walks, and every hold's well is a matter of local pride and occasional litigation.

## Culture And Livelihood

Orderly, conservative, and intensely custom-bound. Shire-law is oral, precedent-driven, and administered at moots where everybody knows everybody's grandfather's dispute. Feuds are conducted through lawsuits, fence-lines, and marriage refusals rather than violence.

Common trades: sheep above all — wool, fleece, cheese, mutton — plus barley, chalk-lime, horse-breeding, and the drove-trade that moves flocks south and wool north.

The downs keep a strong tradition of **grave-songs**: long, formal sung laments used at burials and at the year's memorial days. Down-country singers are sought out well beyond the region, and the older verses are held to be very old indeed.

**Who holds it:** a patchwork of manorial holds under a **shire-reeve** seated at Penmark Hold, checked by **shire-moots** at Marrowmoot. A mounted horse-reeve's troop patrols the down-roads and is, by far-region standards, unusually competent and unusually honest.

## Settlements

- **Marrowmoot** — the shire-town: market, moot-stone, and the region's civic heart.
- **Penmark Hold** — the shire-reeve's manor-town; gentry law and the region's best-kept roads.
- **Wether** — a wool-trade village on the southeastern walks; also where one hires a guide to the mounds, whether or not one should.

## Wilderness Zones

- **The Barrow-Fields** — sacred old ground on the eastern downs. Locals do not dig, camp, or graze there. Barrow-robbers exist and are universally despised.
- **The open downs** — safe, but exposed: no shelter, no water, and weather that arrives without warning.
- **The Mardenflow head-valley** — springs, wet meadow, and the first real river-country south.

## Travel Routes

- **The Salt Road** — the cross-continent caravan track running roughly west to east through Marrowmoot toward the inland salt sea; about a week to cross the region.
- **The Road South** — the wool-drive route running toward the southern forests and the delta.
- **Shire down-roads** — the local network between holds; well-kept and patrolled.

## Local Name For The Last Telling

Folk here call the darkest of the alternate Tellings **the Waning**. It is the same place other regions call by other names; "the Last Telling" is the scholarly term almost nobody uses aloud. See [`../../05_timelines/THE_LAST_TELLING.md`](../../05_timelines/THE_LAST_TELLING.md).

## General Danger And Tone

The safest far region and the most pleasant to cross. Days of green sheep-walk, a hold or village most nights, and law that mostly works.

Real risks: exposure on the high walks, rustlers and road-bandits on the drive-routes, and the mounds. The Barrow-Fields have a hard local reputation, and the standing advice — from shepherds, singers, and the horse-reeve alike — is that whatever is buried there stays buried.
