## Regional Character

The Pale Coast is grey water, high cliffs, shingle coves, and a great deal of weather. Its economy is fish, salt, coastal shipping, and salvage — the last of these being both an honourable trade and, in the wrong coves, a criminal one. The **Pale Road** runs along the clifftops linking the settlements; sea passage down the coast and up the river toward the city is faster and, in good weather, safer.

Coast folk are weathered, blunt, and superstitious in a wholly practical way. Houses carry a knot of salt-grass and a piece of sea-glass at the door and a tide-table chalked by the lintel. The **salt-and-tide rite** — the coastal form of the Mourners' funeral custom, for settling the sea's drowned — is the region's central observance, and coastal Mourners ("salt-mothers") hold real authority. Custom demands a cairn and fresh salt for every drowned body recovered, and an empty place kept at the hearth "for the one the sea sends back."

Coastal Concord ruins are common: drowned lighthouse-stumps, sunken stonework, causeways that run out into the water. Salvaging them is legal, profitable, and generally regarded as slightly disreputable.

# Wrackmouth

**Town · ~1,300 people · the coast's port and capital**

### First Impression
The Pale Road climbs a last headland and the sea opens out, grey and endless, and below in a deep cleft of cliff sits Wrackmouth: a steep town of slate roofs and salt-white timber tumbling down to a stone harbour packed with fishing boats and heavy salt-hulks. Gulls and the reek of fish and brine. Nets, salt-sheds, and the constant boom of surf on the harbour wall. Every house has salt-grass and sea-glass at the door and a chalked tide-table by the lintel — and everyone, at some point in the conversation, glances once at the broken lighthouse on the far headland.

### Public Overview
Wrackmouth is the coast's port and effective capital, where fish, salt, and frontier trade all pass. The Cinder Ledger runs the salt-works, the weigh-house, and the harbour credit, and the price it sets at that scale is felt hundreds of miles inland. The town governs itself through a **harbour-moot** under a harbour-reeve, with the Reachward Compact a thin presence. A strong chapter of the Mourners' Circle maintains the salt-and-tide rites, which matter here more than anywhere: the sea returns its dead constantly, and someone must see to them.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Harbour and Salt-Hulks** — the deep-water cove, the fishing fleet, and the heavy salt ships; departures down the coast and up-river to the city.
- **The Weigh-House** — the salt-scale and credit-office; the town's real power.
- **The Moot-Stair** — the cliff-stair hall where the harbour-moot sits.
- **The Tide-Bell** — the great salt-and-tide shrine and its drowned-bell, rung for the newly drowned.
- **The Wrack** — the rough lower harbour: wreckers, smugglers, fences, and cheap beds. Barely policed.
- **The Gull & Anchor** — the big harbour-inn; trade talk, sea-gossip, and the place to hire a skipper.

### Leadership
A **harbour-reeve** chairs the harbour-moot and speaks for the coast; the post traditionally goes to a retired skipper. About a dozen harbour-watch keep the quays and generally leave the Wrack and the outer coves to themselves. In practice the Cinder Ledger's coastal factor sets policy through the salt price, and the tide-faith commands moral authority on anything touching the drowned.

### Shops and Services
The Gull & Anchor (about 5 sp a night, excellent fish, skippers and guides for hire). The Weigh-House: banking, loans, the salt market, ship's stores, and **passage to the city by sea and river** — the coast's distinctive route. A full harbour market: chandler, sailmaker, smith, a healer's house with folk medicine and a tide-priest capable of minor healing. Boats and crews for hire. The Tide-Bell performs the salt-and-tide rites.

### Laws and Customs
Harbour-moot law on the quays; very little law anywhere else. Salt-grass and sea-glass at the door; read the tide-table before doing business with anyone; never put to sea while the drowned-bell is tolling; leave the sea its due. Wrecking and unlicensed salvage-smuggling are crimes on paper and commonplace in fact, tolerated so long as the customary cut reaches the right hands.

### Local Colour
A weigh-house queue of furious fisherfolk comparing this season's salt price with last season's; the whole town falling silent, mid-errand, when the drowned-bell rings; a skipper who will name a fair price to the city and then argue about the weather for an hour.

# Cobble Strand

**Village · ~150 people · a shingle-cove fishing village**

### First Impression
The cliff-road drops to a grey shingle cove and a huddle of tarred cottages with upturned-boat roofs, fish-racks, and a little salt-and-tide shrine at the high-tide line. Above, on the headland, the broken stump of an old Concord lighthouse stands black against the sky. The folk are lean, salt-cured, and grave, and they keep an empty place at every hearth "for the one the sea sends back." At the strand's edge, a row of small cairns marks the drowned, and someone has left fresh salt on each of them.

### Public Overview
Cobble Strand fishes the cove and works the near salt-pans, scraping a hard living under a salt price set somewhere else by someone richer. Its faith is the coastal Mourners' tradition in its purest form — the salt-and-tide rites kept by the village's salt-mother. The village has always lived close to the dead, because the sea is always giving them back. It reveres and slightly fears the ruined lighthouse above it, which the old songs call the light that "called the drowned home."

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Strand and Cairns** — the shingle beach, the boats, and the drowned-cairns along the tide-line.
- **The Tide-Shrine** — the salt-mother's shrine at the high-water mark; the village's heart.
- **The Boat-Hall** — the common hall, built from an upturned hulk, where news and grief are shared.
- **The Lamp Stair** — the cliff-path up to the ruined lighthouse on the headland.
- **The Cove Caves** — sea-caves under the headland, tide-flooded and dangerous, and used by people who prefer not to be seen.

### Leadership
The **salt-mother**, keeper of the tide-shrine, leads in everything that matters: the rites, the drowned, the old songs. The **headman**, usually the eldest fisher, speaks for the village's worldly affairs and to the port town's moot, and defers to her on the dead.

### Shops and Services
No inn — a respected guest is given hearth-room and fish-stew, and the hospitality is genuine and earned. Fish, salt, and tide-lore to trade; net-mending and boat repair; a folk-healer, plus the salt-mother's rite-aid, which is the best care on the coast outside the port. Boats and a guide can be hired for the coves and the near sea, and are essential for either.

### Laws and Customs
Custom is law; the salt-mother and the headman settle disputes; there is no militia and the sea is the great authority. A cairn and fresh salt for every drowned. An empty hearth-place kept for the returned. Nothing is taken from a wreck without the salt-mother's rite first — a rule wreckers break, to the village's lasting grief. The village shelters its own and distrusts outsiders who make light of the tide.

### Local Colour
A net-wife who is blunt to the point of rudeness and unfailingly generous; a fisher-lad who has stopped going out since his father drowned; the salt-mother deciding, over a long silence, whether a stranger has earned an old song.
