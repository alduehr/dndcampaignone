## Regional Character

The Vale is the first genuinely pleasant country a traveller from the fens sees: the Ammet river running through orchard slopes, cider-presses, nut-groves, wool, and grain. The **South Road**, an old Concord causeway, runs its length toward the city, and the Vale lives partly off the traffic — traders, carters, and above all pilgrims.

Vale culture is settled, ritualised, and a little pleased with itself. Households press a fresh clay star into the lintel at each new moon. Funeral custom here is elaborate and public, and the Vale's shrine-towns have built centuries of reputation on the promise that the dead rest well in this valley. Speaking loudly about a funeral that went badly is regarded less as bad news than as bad manners.

Governance is local: a **harvest-moot** of orchard-masters and shrine-keepers, chaired by a moot-reeve, with a handful of moot-wardens for law. The **Mourners' Circle** is stronger and older here than anywhere in the northwest, and their eldest carries moral authority that rivals the moot's political authority. The **Cinder Ledger** holds a great many Vale mortgages, which is the region's other permanent political fact.

# Orchardmere

**Town · ~1,600 people · the Vale's hub**

### First Impression
The South Road crests a rise and the Vale opens below: orchards in red-gold autumn rows, a broad bright lake, and a town of mellow stone and cider-presses under woodsmoke and the sweet rot-smell of pressing apples. Bells ring the harvest-hours. People are well-fed, well-dressed for a frontier, and unhurried; they nod to strangers and watch them a beat too long. Garlands of dried apple-blossom hang over every door, and a small fresh clay star sits pressed into each lintel.

### Public Overview
Orchardmere is the richest town a traveller from the fens is likely to have seen. Cider, nuts, wool, and grain go south to the city and the money comes back. It governs the Vale through the harvest-moot and defers, in all matters concerning the dead, to the Mourners' Circle at the Old Circle. The town is proud of its orchards, prouder of its rites, and extremely conscious of its own reputation.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Moot-Hall** — timber-and-stone hall on the green where the harvest-moot sits; the Vale's seat of power.
- **The Pressyards** — cider- and nut-presses and their deep cellars along the lake; the town's wealth and most of its labour.
- **The Old Circle** — the ancient grave-garden and shrine, the oldest in this part of Orrun and the town's spiritual heart.
- **The Cider Star** — the fine inn, named for the lintel-stars; where pilgrims, traders, and travelling scholars meet.
- **The Counting-Yard** — the Cinder Ledger's Vale office, credit-counter, and buying-desk.
- **The Lakeside** — quays, fishing boats, and the pilgrim-ferries that carry the devout toward the shrine track.

### Leadership
A **moot-reeve** chairs the harvest-moot and speaks for the Vale — an administrative post, elected by orchard-masters, concerned above all with trade and reputation. Roughly half a dozen **moot-wardens** keep order. Real justice here is communal and reputational: being run out of a shrine-town is a heavier sentence than a fine.

### Shops and Services
The Cider Star (about 6 sp a night, good food, better cider). Full market: smith, cooper, fletcher, a fine chandler, a healer's house with folk medicine and a hedge-priest capable of minor blessings and healing. Horses and carts for the South Road. The counting-yard offers banking, loans, credit south to the city, and buys curios and old salvage over the counter. The Old Circle performs funeral rites and grief-counsel.

### Laws and Customs
Harvest-moot law. A fresh clay star at every lintel, renewed at the new moon. Grave-meddling and relic-theft are punished ferociously by custom — the gibbet at the crossroads is not decorative. Speaking loudly of failed rites is considered ill-bred and unlucky, and earns cold stares even from people who agree with you.

### Local Colour
A press-master who can tell you which orchard a cup came from; moot sessions that run for hours over a boundary hedge; pilgrims buying blessed cider by the flask before the climb to the shrine.

# Saint Veddow's Rest

**Town · ~700 residents plus pilgrims · the Vale's great pilgrimage**

### First Impression
The pilgrim-track switchbacks up a green hill crowned by an old, beautiful shrine of pale stone — finer and far older than anything in the fen country. Bells, incense, and the murmur of crowds. The town below is small but rich on pilgrim coin: hostels, candle-sellers, relic-stalls hawking saint's-bones and blessed earth, and lines of the faithful climbing barefoot to lay flowers at the tomb.

### Public Overview
The town venerates a Frontier-Age holy woman said to have settled the restless dead with a touch; her hill-tomb is the oldest intact Threshold shrine for a hundred miles and the Vale's great pilgrimage. The Mourners' Circle holds the shrine. Unusually, a resident chapter of the Ashen Wardens is stationed here permanently, guarding the inner tomb and aiding pilgrims — a posting the order does not explain and outsiders find odd. The whole local economy runs on the shrine's reputation.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Shrine Crown** — the pale hill-shrine: pilgrim hall, flower-altar, and the sealed door of the inner tomb, which is not open to visitors.
- **The Warden House** — the resident Ashen Wardens' fortified house beside the shrine; sanctuary, aid, and a great deal of quiet watchfulness.
- **Relic Row** — stalls and shops selling saint's-bones, blessed earth, candles, tokens, and pilgrim gear. Buyer beware.
- **The Pilgrim Hostels** — lodging from bare dormitory to genuinely comfortable; where rumour and confession circulate.
- **The Lower Cells** — quiet rooms where the keepers tend pilgrims whose grief has gone badly. Visitors are not encouraged.

### Leadership
The **shrine-keeper**, an elder of the Mourners' Circle, governs shrine and town by sacred authority. The resident Warden chapter holds independent authority over the inner tomb and answers to its own order rather than to the town — a jurisdictional split that occasionally produces spectacular arguments.

### Shops and Services
Pilgrim hostels from about 2 sp (dormitory) to 8 sp (fine); plentiful and safe. Relic Row sells candles, blessed earth, relics of extremely variable authenticity, and pilgrim supplies. The Warden House offers true rite-aid, sanctuary, and minor healing magic — the best healing in the Vale. The shrine performs funeral rites and grief-blessing.

### Laws and Customs
Sacred law: on the hill, the shrine-keeper's word is final. The inner tomb is forbidden to all but the Ashen Wardens, and forcing it is sacrilege rather than mere trespass. Pilgrims climb the last furlong barefoot, lay a flower rather than a coin at the altar, and speak no ill of the dead on the hill. Relic-selling is technically frowned upon and enormously profitable — a hypocrisy that a sharp visitor will notice within the hour.

### Local Colour
A relic-monger who can produce three different saints' fingerbones before breakfast; a hosteler who has heard every family's story twice; a pilgrim crowd that goes very quiet, all at once, when a bell rings out of turn.

# Tilbrook

**Village · ~250 people · the Vale's working mill village**

### First Impression
After the fens the Ammet valley feels almost gentle: a brown river turning a big mossy mill-wheel, a huddle of thatch and daub, geese, and the steady knock of the mill. But the gentleness is thin. Cottages need mending, the children are lean, and there is a row of empty houses at the village edge with chalked marks on the doors. The folk here are blunt where the shrine-town people are smooth, and they size up a stranger by what work they could do.

### Public Overview
Tilbrook mills the Vale's grain and grows what the orchard-towns don't — a working village of millers, ploughmen, and a few weavers. It owes most of its land to the Cinder Ledger, which has been foreclosing as harvests tighten. Its grief-keeping is plain: no grand shrine, just a hedged grave-field and an old woman who sings the passings. Tilbrook says out loud what the shrine-towns will not, on almost every subject, which is the main reason people either like it enormously or avoid it.

### Districts and Notable Areas
- **The Mill** — the big Ammet water-mill; the village's heart and its largest single debt.
- **The Grave-Field** — a plain hedged burial ground with a grave-singer's cottage beside it.
- **The Marks** — the row of foreclosed cottages with chalk on the doors.
- **The Plough & Star** — the ale-house; blunt talk and unusually reliable news.

### Leadership
No reeve. The **miller** speaks for the village to the moot and the bank, holding the mill and most of the debt, and is slowly being crushed between the two. The **grave-singer** holds the village's spiritual authority and is not shy about using it.

### Shops and Services
The Plough & Star: cheap lodging (about 3 sp), plain food, ale, and news. A smith, the mill (flour and rough repair), a hedge-healer. Carts south to the Vale hub or north toward the fens. No banking beyond the debt-book, and no healing magic.

### Laws and Customs
Compact and moot law in name; in practice the miller and custom settle disputes, and the moot-wardens rarely come. Plain rites: a loaf left on a new grave for the dead's journey. The village shelters its own from debt-enforcers whenever it can, and considers this a virtue rather than a crime.

### Local Colour
An ale-wife who will tell you exactly what she thinks of the shrine-towns; a foreclosed widow living in a neighbour's outbuilding; village children who can name every family that has left in the last five years.
